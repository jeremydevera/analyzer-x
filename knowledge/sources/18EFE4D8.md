## What it is

A short article by John Ehlers, who wrote the MESA8 program and runs trading websites (the code is dated 2006). He argues that any indicator with a fixed length goes wrong when the market keeps changing, so you should first measure the market's dominant cycle and then set the indicator lengths from it. He says a plain Fourier transform is too blurry to find that cycle, then shows a trick from a 1984 paper by Kay and Demeure that sharpens it, with EasyLanguage code. He ends by admitting the sharpened method still has weaknesses and that his own MESA8 program separates two close cycles better. There are no trades, no profit figures and no backtests in it.

## Everything it teaches

1. Fixed-length indicators are the wrong tool when the market changes.
> "It is intrinsically wrong to use a 14 bar RSI, a 9 bar Stochastic, a 5/25 Double Moving Average crossover, or any other fixed -length indicator when the market conditions are variable."
Where: the opening sentence.

2. Market conditions are always changing; he treats this as settled.
> "That market conditions are continuously changing is not even a subject of debate."
Where: first paragraph, just after the cruise-control comparison.

3. Making a moving average adapt to volatility is one known way to adapt, but it is only one.
> "Volatility-based nonlinear m oving averages are just one example to adapt to market changes."
Where: first paragraph, "There have been a number of attempts to adapt..."

4. His own answer: measure the dominant cycle, then tune indicators to that period or a fraction of it.
> "my answer to the question of how to adapt to changing conditions is to first measure the dominant market cycle and then tune the various indicators to that cycle period, or a fraction of it."
Where: first paragraph, "Coming from an information theory background..."

5. An RSI should work best when its length is half of the cycle period, because a full cycle then swings it end to end.
> "an RSI theoretically performs best when the computation period is just one half of a cycle period."
Where: the "For example" sentence in the first paragraph. He says "theoretically", and gives no test of it.

6. Measuring a market cycle is hard because you must solve for frequency, amplitude and phase at once, and the data is noisy.
> "Not only the problem of simultaneously solving for frequency, amplitude, and phase to arrive at an accurate estimate, but also one must realized the measurement is being made in a low signal to noise environment."
Where: "Make no mistake. Measuring market cycles is difficult."

7. The measuring method must react to cycles that keep changing, without creating false signals of its own.
> "one must be concerned with the responsiveness of the measuring technique to capture the cycle periods that are continuously changing without introducing transient artifacts into the measurement."
Where: same paragraph as lesson 6.

8. His objection to Fourier transforms is their lack of resolution, meaning they blur close cycles together.
> "I have long railed against the use of Fourier Transforms for use in estimating market cycles because of their lack of resolution."
Where: the paragraph about spectrum estimation techniques.

9. How to read a spectrum chart: the tallest point marks the cycle, and a narrow spike means high resolution.
> "The frequency having the highest amplitude identifies the measured cycle."
Where: "I'll show you what I mean. Figure 1 represents a typical spectrum measurement."

10. A blurry (low-resolution) measurement can merge two nearby cycles so you cannot tell they are two.
> "if we have a low resolution measurement technique, the width of the spectral line is very broad, two closely space d cycles could be averaged together and you could not identify them"
Where: Figure 1 paragraph, "On the other hand..."

11. He draws the spectrum as a colour heatmap under the price chart, so a sharp measurement shows as a thin line and a blurry one as a blob.
> "I convert the amplitude to colors ranging from white hot to ice cold, thro ugh red hot."
Where: the Figure 1 paragraph, "On the right hand vertical scale of Figure 1 you see a color bar."
> "If the spe ctral line is very wide, then it appears as a yellow blob in the heatmap display."
Where: the same paragraph, last sentences.

12. His plain Fourier transform of IBM was too blurry to trade on.
> "There may be some cyclic activity, but it is so blurry that it cannot be useful for trading."
Where: the paragraph under Figure 2, "Now I think you see why I have advised against the use of Fourier Transforms."

13. Judging which method is sharper by eye can mislead: two spectra that look very different can be the same method seen through a transformation.
> "One would conclude from this visual inspection that the Bartlett spectrum is vastly inferior with respect to resolution."
Where: the Kay and Demeure paragraph before Figure 3.
> "Nonetheless, the point is that the two techniques have the same resolution regardless of how they look in a visual inspection when the transform is taken into account."
Where: the paragraph after Figure 3.

14. Applying that transformation to the Fourier spectrum, with 20 decibels (100 to 1) of boost on the peak, makes the dominant cycle visible.
> "I applied it to the DFT spectrum estimator using a 20 decibel amplification (100:1) of the peak spectral line."
Where: "Armed with this transform..."

15. A cycle longer than 50 bars means the market is trending, so he only looks at cycles from 8 to 50 bars.
> "In my opinion, when the cycle period exceeds 50 bars, the market is in a trend and the cycle measurement can be of little help."
Where: the paragraph before Figure 4. This is his opinion; no test is given.

16. Clean the data before the transform: remove the trend with a 40-bar high-pass filter.
> "Therefore, I detrend the data by first passing it through a 40 bar highpass filter."
Where: the preprocessing paragraph before Figure 5's code.

17. Cleaning matters because unwanted frequencies create false cross-terms in the maths.
> "Preprocessing is important for spectral analysis to avoid having undesired frequency components introduc e cross products in the multiply steps of the analysis."
Where: the first sentence of the preprocessing paragraph.

18. Rejecting long cycles first makes the error from cutting the data window mid-cycle small.
> "Since long cycles are rejected by the highpass filter, the end effect errors of having noninteger cycles within th e data window is relatively small."
Where: the preprocessing paragraph.

19. The high-pass filter is gentle enough to keep cycles out to 50 bars.
> "The highpass filter is only a 2 pole filter, and so components out to our 50 bar plotting limit are passed."
Where: the preprocessing paragraph.

20. Very short cycles (2 to 4 bars) are removed with a small smoothing filter.
> "I eliminate the 2 bar, 3 bar, and 4 bar cycle components by low pass filtering in a 6 element FIR filter."
Where: the preprocessing paragraph.

21. The best way he found to get one number for the dominant cycle is a centre-of-gravity average over the spectrum.
> "My experience is that this center of gravity approach yields the smoothest and most reliable estimate of the dominant cycle period."
Where: "After the DFT portion I also show how to extract the dominant cycle..." This is his experience, not a measured comparison.

22. You do not need to draw the spectrum to get the dominant cycle; it is computed anyway.
> "However, extraction of the dominant cycle does not depend on spectrum plotting."
Where: the paragraph about converting the code to other platforms.

23. Most of the time the data holds one strong cycle, which is why a single dominant cycle is a workable idea.
> "Simultaneous cycles are present with a low probability."
Where: the paragraph just before the code listing. He judges this by looking at one chart (Figure 4, IBM).
> "Therefore, the concept of using a dominant cycle to tune indicators is valid, or at least suff iciently valid to be used to your advantage to dynamically adjust your indicators."
Where: the same paragraph, last sentence.

24. Test a spectrum method on made-up data whose answer you know, not by eye.
> "Kay and Demeure conclude that means other than visual inspection should be used to assess the resolution of spectral estimators."
Where: right after Figure 5's code listing.
> "One approach is to create known data that contains two close ly spaced cycles and see if the spectral estimator can discern whether there are two cycles present or not."
Where: same paragraph.

25. On a series with a pure 20-bar and a pure 24-bar cycle, the sharpened Fourier method could not tell there were two cycles, while MESA8 could.
> "In a nutshell, it does not have sufficient resolution to identify that both these closely spaced cycles are present."
Where: the paragraph about Figure 5 (labelled Figure 6 in the caption) after the code.
> "Clearly, MESA8 does, in fact, have sufficient resolution to identify both cycles as be present."
Where: the next paragraph, "I also app lied MESA8 to this same data..." This is the author comparing his own product on one made-up series.

26. Even the sharpened method has limits: unsure in noisy data, needs a lot of history, slow to react, and averages mixed cycles together.
> "I am not convinced of its accuracy in low signal to noise environments."
Where: the closing paragraph, "Although the Kay and Demeure transform improve the resolution..."
> "it still takes a reasonably amount of historical data to make a measurement."
Where: same paragraph.
> "That means it can be sluggish in response to rapid changes and mixed cycle periods within the observation window and get averaged together even when their periods are widely spaced."
Where: same paragraph.
> "Still, it is better than driving in the fog."
Where: end of the closing paragraph.

27. Code detail: the listing reads the middle of each bar's range as the price, over a 50-bar window.
> "Inputs: Price((H+L)/2), Window(50), ShowDC(False);"
Where: the start of the EasyLanguage listing (Figure 5).

## Numbers and rules it gives

1. Example fixed-length indicators he calls wrong: RSI 14, Stochastic 9, moving-average crossover 5/25.
> "a 14 bar RSI, a 9 bar Stochastic, a 5/25 Double Moving Average crossover"

2. RSI length rule: half a cycle period.
> "when the computation period is just one half of a cycle period"

3. Cycle range shown: 8 to 50 bars; longer than 50 means trend.
> "when the cycle period exceeds 50 bars, the market is in a trend"

4. Boost: 20 decibels, which he says is 100 to 1, on the peak line.
> "using a 20 decibel amplification (100:1) of the peak spectral line"

5. Detrending: 40-bar high-pass, 2-pole.
> "a 40 bar highpass filter"

6. Short cycles removed: 2, 3 and 4 bars, by a 6-element FIR low-pass.
> "low pass filtering in a 6 element FIR filter"

7. Data window and price: 50 bars, price is (high + low) / 2.
> "Inputs: Price((H+L)/2), Window(50), ShowDC(False);"

8. High-pass coefficient in the code, with a cutoff of 40 bars.
> "alpha1 = (1 - Sine(360/40))/Cosine(360/40);"

9. High-pass filter step in the code.
> "HP = .5*(1 + alpha1)*(Price - Price[1]) + alpha1*HP[1];"

10. The 6-element smoothing filter weights are 1, 2, 3, 3, 2, 1 divided by 12.
> "CleanedData = (HP + 2*HP[1] + 3*HP[2] + 3*HP[3] + 2*HP[4] + HP[5])/12;"

11. The first 5 bars are not filtered (price is copied through).
> "If CurrentBar <= 5 Then Begin HP = Price; CleanedData = Price; End;"

12. The Fourier step is tried for every period from 8 to 50 bars.
> "For Period = 8 to 50 Begin CosinePart[Period] = 0; SinePart[Period] = 0;"

13. Power at each period is the cosine part squared plus the sine part squared.
> "Pwr[Period] = CosinePart[Period]*CosinePart[Period] + SinePart[Period]*SinePart[Period];"

14. Normalisation to decibels, relative to the strongest period. By my arithmetic from this formula (not stated in the text), the value is 0 at the strongest period and 20 where the power is zero, so a LOW number means a strong cycle.
> "DB[Period] = -10*LOG(.01 / (1 - .99*Pwr[Period] / MaxPwr))/Log(10);"

15. The decibel value is capped at 20.
> "If DB[Period] > 20 then DB[Period] = 20;"

16. Centre-of-gravity rule for the dominant cycle: only periods with DB under 3 count, each weighted by (3 minus DB).
> "If DB[Period] < 3 Then Begin Num = Num + Period*(3 - DB[Period]); Denom = Denom + (3 - DB[Period]); End;"

17. The dominant cycle is the weighted average of those periods.
> "If Denom <> 0 then DominantCycle = Num/Denom;"

18. The heatmap colour switches at a DB of 10.
> "If DB[Period] > 10 Then Begin Color1 = 255*(2 - DB[Period]/10); Color2 = 0; End;"

19. The test series: one pure 20-bar cycle and one pure 24-bar cycle of equal size.
> "a pure 20 bar cycle and a pure 24 bar cycle of equal amplitude"

20. The Kay and Demeure link between the two spectra: the text says that when the Bartlett value reaches 1, the other spectrum's line goes to infinity. The formula's own symbols did not survive in the text file (it reads "Bartlett MUSIC SS  1 1"), so the exact formula is not available here.
> "when the Barlett spectrum is 1, the denominator of the transformation goes to zero and the spectral line of the MUSIC spectrum goes to in finity."

21. Source cited.
> "Proceedings IEEE, Vol 72, Dec 1984, pp1815-1816"

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. "A cycle-length-matched RSI beats a fixed 14-bar RSI." Port the listing, compute the dominant cycle from past bars only, then run RSI with a length of half that cycle against RSI(14) on the same crypto 15m, 30m, 1h and 4h bars. Charge the exchange's fee and each minute's cost, judge on days not used to choose the rule, and compare with random entries. The text gives no result for this, so it is an untested idea.
2. "Fixed-length indicators go wrong when conditions change." Compare a 5/25 crossover with a version whose lengths follow the dominant cycle, across calm and busy months, after costs.
3. "When the dominant cycle is over 50 bars the market is trending, so cycle tuning is of little help." Measure how often the estimate sits above 50 on crypto bars, then check whether returns after such bars behave differently (for example, whether a trend rule does better there than a reversal rule).
4. "There is only one strong cycle most of the time." Count how many separate peaks the transformed spectrum has on each bar of crypto data, and report the share of bars with exactly one.
5. "The centre-of-gravity estimate is the smoothest and most reliable." Compare the bar-to-bar jumping of the centre-of-gravity estimate with simply taking the strongest period, on real bars and on synthetic cycles with known answers.
6. "The sharpened Fourier method cannot separate a 20-bar and a 24-bar cycle." Easy unit check: build the two-cycle series, run the ported code, and see whether two peaks appear.
7. "The method is sluggish when cycles change quickly." Feed it a made-up series whose cycle length drifts, and measure how many bars late the estimate follows.
8. Before any of this, check that the ported code reads only past bars at each step (the listing's [n] index appears to count bars back, which is my reading of EasyLanguage, not something the text says), because a hidden look-ahead would make any test look good.

## What I did not read, and doubts

- I read all 2,353 words. I did not read the seven figures, because they are pictures and are not in the text. The article leans on them (the IBM heatmaps, the MUSIC and Bartlett curves), so the visual claims are not checkable from the text.
- The Kay and Demeure formula did not come out of the PDF as readable text, so the note does not state it. I could not render the PDF pages here (no page renderer installed).
- There are no extra tutorial summaries for this source (no 18EFE4D8-tut files). The earlier saved summary (sections\18EFE4D8.html) agrees with this note.
- Marketing flags: the author wrote the MESA8 program and runs trading websites (the text does not say whether they are sold), and the paper ends with a comparison in which his own product wins on one made-up series. The two figures he redrew are described as not precisely accurate: "I have taken artistic license and have redrawn them."
- The strongest claims are opinion ("in my opinion", "my experience", "theoretically") with no numbers behind them. No profit, win rate, drawdown or cost figure appears anywhere, so nothing in it shows that cycle-tuned indicators make money.
- The code is from 2006 and was written for one charting platform; it uses a 50-bar window and several fixed numbers, so any port to crypto bars needs its own testing.
