## What it is

This is the front page of a personal blog by a quant (computer-driven) trader, and it holds ten posts in a row. Most posts are about tactical asset allocation, which means picking among about ten ETFs once a month by momentum (recent price rise), and the posts are mostly R and Python code with a few paragraphs between. The strongest ideas for us are about how a backtest is done: when you enter (next open instead of the same close), how you measure a "month" (calendar month ends versus 21-day chunks), and how the start day changes the result ("timing luck"). It also has a rank-test method for comparing two return series that are far from a bell curve, and a book review. Nothing here is about crypto, intraday data, trading fees or stop-losses; the data is daily and monthly ETF data. One post (Aug 2, 2021) also has a short fetch-tool summary file saved beside it (see the end); it is not the author's words and is not quoted here.

18. The recipe for a next-day-open test: compute open-to-open returns, then shift them one day back.
    > "Compute open-to-open returns. Lag them by negative one."
    Where: Aug 2, 2021 post, "So, the trick here, first, in English is this".

19. The shift of minus one looks like look-ahead but is not, because the weights come from the close and the return is from the next open.
    > "given my weights for data at the close of June 30, what would my open-to-open returns have been if I entered on the open of July 1st, instead of the June 30 close?"
    Where: Aug 2, 2021 post, the paragraph on "lag of negative one implies the potential for lookahead bias".

20. After that shift the dates are one day off, so shift the portfolio returns back by one day.
    > "Of course, this sets the return dates off by one day"
    Where: Aug 2, 2021 post, same paragraph.

21. Trading the next open instead of the close cost about 2% a year, which is a fifth of the return.
    > "performance deterioration to the tune of about 2% per year. While the strategy may still be solid, a loss of 20% of the CAGR means that the other risk/reward statistics suffer proportionally as well."
    Where: Aug 2, 2021 post, the paragraph after the stratStats table.

22. A strategy with high turnover is sensitive to exactly how and when it is executed.
    > "this is a strategy that is fairly sensitive to the exact execution due to its fairly high turnover."
    Where: Aug 2, 2021 post, same paragraph.

23. Turnover can be cut by lowering the weight of relative momentum, and the later code has a switch for it.

24. A good book on portfolio building measures how much risk the client can take through three traits: risk aversion, loss aversion and reflection.
    > "The three types of behavioral risk traits are risk aversion"
    Where: post "A Review of Modern Asset Allocation For Wealth Management, by David M. Berns, PhD" (Oct 5, 2020), second paragraph.

25. Good portfolios should have high skew (cut losers, let winners run) and low kurtosis (rare huge moves).
    > "one wants to maximize portfolio skew (namely, cutting losers and letting winners run"
    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, the paragraph on "higher portfolio moments". The same paragraph also says "while minimizing kurtosis (tail risk is painful!)".

26. Optimizing on skew and kurtosis before the fact is heavy work; using a simple rule after the fact is cheaper.
    > "performing optimization on third and fourth portfolio moments *ex ante* is most likely much more computationally expensive in runtime than deploying a heuristic on it *ex post*."
    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, same section.

27. Heavy models leave far fewer test runs to check how fragile the result is to small parameter changes.
    > "which means far fewer simulations to measure portfolio robustness to perturbations in parameter settings (EG lookback periods, rebalance dates, etc.)"
    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, end of the co-skewness paragraph.

28. Before adding an asset, check if the others already copy it: if a mix of them tracks it within 5%, it adds little and can confuse an optimizer.
    > "if the tracking error is lower than 5% for the asset considered to be added to the universe as expressed through a linear combination of assets in the existing universe"
    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, the paragraph on Mimicking Portfolio Tracking Error (MPTE).

29. Split assets into "performance" assets (high return, high swings) and "diversifying" assets (lower return, smoother).
    > "performance assets (high return at the cost of high volatility, low skew, high kurtosis), and diversifying assets (lower return but that smoothen the portfolio trajectory by reducing overall portfolio volatility and kurtosis)"
    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, "The other fantastic idea presented here".

30. A client has to be able to stick with the ride, so a better risk-adjusted portfolio (possibly leveraged up) is better.

31. Do not assume what an asset did over decades it will keep doing; the author disagrees with the book's stationarity test.

    Where: Oct 5, 2020 post, "Next comes the idea with which I have a philosophical disagreement with".

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

Costs: not covered as a charge. No fee, spread or slippage is subtracted from any return in any code shown. Turnover is measured (the "two-way turnover" output) and called a source of risk: "this is a strategy that is fairly sensitive to the exact execution due to its fairly high turnover." The only price-realism step is trading the next day's open instead of the close, which cost about 2% a year (a fifth of the return) in his example. Fees appear only as "management fees" the author did not get paid on, and "transaction risks" of shorting VXX are named without numbers.

Overfitting: the word never appears. The nearest ideas are robustness checks by moving parameters: 21 different start days, a 21-day month versus real month ends, equal and reversed filter weights. He concludes the 12-4-2-1 filter "was designed around the idea of the end-of-month effect, or at least, not stress-tested for different trading days within the month." He also warns that advanced machine learning did worse and quotes "Auto-ML = auto-bankrupt."

Testing on unseen data: not covered. There is no holdout, no walk-forward test and no split into a period used to pick and a period used to judge. Everything is full-history, in-sample. The only statistics beyond return tables are rank tests (Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon) on SVIX against XIV and SVXY returns, and a rolling one-year version of the Wilcoxon test.

Position size: not covered for single trades. Sizing here means portfolio weights across ETFs: minimum-variance weights with each chosen asset between 5% and 100%, equal weight, inverse volatility and inverse variance, and a crash-protection share set by the canary pair. There is no risk-per-trade rule.

Leverage: one finding. Multiplying the risk-on allocation by 1.5 or 2 made Sharpe and Calmar ratios go down in his test ("as leverage during aggressive allocations increases, characteristics such as the Sharpe and Calmar ratio actually *decrease*"). The book review adds that a better risk-adjusted portfolio can be leveraged up. No margin, liquidation or funding is discussed.

Stops: not covered. There are no stop-loss or take-profit rules. Protection comes from a monthly canary signal (VWO and BND momentum) that moves money to bonds or cash, and from the remark that volatility products must be trend-followed because XIV lost 95% in a day.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. Trading one candle late costs a lot for fast, high-turnover rules (the source: about 2% a year, a fifth of the return, for monthly ETF rotation). Test: for our strategy grid on crypto 15m to 1d, compare a fill at the signal candle's own close against a fill at the next candle's open, from the 1-minute store, and report the profit drop per trade. Crypto trades all day, so the gap will be smaller than for ETFs; the size of the drop is the answer.
2. "Timing luck": results move a lot with the start day. Test: rebuild our 1h, 4h and 1d candles from 1-minute data with the day boundary shifted (for example 1 to 23 hours), re-run the same signals, and see how widely profit and win rate spread. A rule whose result collapses on a shifted boundary is calendar luck.
3. "Formal month ends beat synthetic 21-day months" (9 of 12 years in his table). Crypto analogue: candles aligned to the exchange's boundary against rolling windows of the same length from minute data, same signals, same unseen days.
4. Day-of-month or end-of-month effect (claimed strong for his ETF filter). Test: group our existing trades by day of month and hour of day and check whether profit differs beyond what the random-entry runs show.
5. More leverage lowers the risk-adjusted result. Test: the same entries at 5x, 10x and 20x (the app trades 20x) on our unseen days, reporting Sharpe-like and worst-run figures, with liquidation distance counted.
6. Fancy machine learning loses to simple rules. Test: compare the repo's learned-formula groups with simple signals on the same unseen days after fees, per coin.
7. A weighted momentum score that favours the latest month (12-4-2-1) beats equal or reversed weights, mostly in bad markets. Test: a weekly cross-section of liquid coins with 1-month, 3-month, 6-month and 12-month returns (in days), top 5 with positive score, compared across the three weightings on unseen days.
8. A canary pair controls how much risk to take. Test: use BTC and one more coin as the canary and scale or skip alt entries by whether both have positive combined momentum; compare unseen profit with and without.
9. Return series with huge tails (kurtosis 85) cannot be compared with t-tests. Test: compute skew and kurtosis of per-trade returns for our rows and check that a rank test and a bootstrap agree on which rows beat random.
10. "Tracking error under 5% means the asset adds nothing." Test: constrained least-squares of each coin's returns on a basket of others; count coins under 5% tracking error, to see how many of our coins are really one bet.
11. Past decades do not repeat. Test: compare each coin's older-window return distribution to its unseen-window one with a rank test and count how often they differ.
