## What it is

Quantitative Finance Stack Exchange is a free question-and-answer site where traders, students and researchers ask about quant finance and vote on the answers. This pull is 60 popular questions, mostly from 2011 to 2022, about how to test a trading idea honestly (data snooping, holding data back, walk-forward), what trading really costs (spread, slippage, market impact), how big to bet (Kelly and its rivals), stop losses, and a little on crypto. It is a forum, not a textbook: each answer is one person's view, the votes say how many readers liked it, and some answers are written by people selling the product they recommend. Most of it is about US stocks and options; only five questions are about crypto, and none are about perpetual futures, funding or high leverage.

### Overfitting and multiple testing

1. If you keep changing settings until something works, you will find a model that worked last year and will not make money next year.
   > "Did you fiddle with too many parameters for too long, eventually finding a model that worked great last year but won't make a penny in the future?"
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 43 votes.
2. Expect real profits to be only part of what the backtest showed.
   > "Your actual profits will likely be only a fraction of your simulated profits."
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 43 votes.
3. Even a good idea wears out; ask how long it will keep working.
   > "Even if your model back-tests well, what is it's half-life? How long will it be tradable? Very few ideas work forever."
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 43 votes.
4. The person testing is the biggest risk: the more you want a result, the harder you must try to break it.
   > "YOU are the biggest risk to the process."
   > "The more you want something to be true, the more you have to kick the tires."
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 13 votes.
5. First check whether the idea predicts correctly, before looking at money made.
   > "make prediction and measure their success (accuracy etc. NOT P&L)"
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 13 votes.
6. Backtest as little as you can and do not keep tweaking; if the result disappoints, the mistake is in your idea, not in the settings.
   > "Resist the urge to tweak, and if you must - only do it with some careful thought and justification."
   > "If its not the money machine that you expected then you made a mistake upstream"
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 13 votes.
7. One fund made traders hand in models weeks early so they could be tested again on days the trader had not seen.
   > "I worked for one hedge fund that required traders to submit their models weeks before production so they could be backtested again without the benefit of hindsight."
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 12 votes.
8. Searching long enough always finds something that worked by luck.
   > "Surely if we search long enough for working strategies we will end up finding one."
   — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), question text (37 votes).
9. Data snooping means using the same data more than once to choose; it is a different problem from training versus testing.
   > "Data snooping occurs when a given set of data is used more than once for purposes of inference or model selection."
   > "Strictly speaking, data snooping is not the same as in-sample vs out-of-sample model selection and testing"
   — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 23 votes (accepted).
10. Test enough models on the same held-back data and one will look good by chance, even if every model was fitted carefully.
    > "If the number of models is high enough, there is a non-negligible probability that the predictions provided by one model will be considered good."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 23 votes (accepted).
11. Correct for the number of tests, but the simplest correction (Bonferroni) is too strict; Benjamini-Hochberg, White and Romano-Wolf are the better tools, Benjamini-Hochberg the easiest to read.
    > "It turns out that this criterion is too stringent."
    > "That's where Benjamini-Hochberg , White , and Romano-Wolf kick in."
    > "I recommend Benjamini-Hochberg first, which is both easier to read and truly seminal."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 23 votes (accepted).
12. Fewer settings means less room to overfit.
    > "less degrees of freedom in your model equates to less chance for overfitting."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
13. A setting whose result jumps when you nudge it is probably overfit.
    > "Parameters that result in sharp changes in expected prediction error will be more open to the risk of overfitting."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
14. A rule with no economic reason behind it should work on many assets, not one.
    > "if the model has no fundamental basis, then it should be applicable to a wide number of assets."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
15. Cross-validation and using several models together reduce curve fitting.
    > "ensemble methods (using multiple models can outperform just one and further reduce the curve-fitting problem)"
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
16. Check for survivors-only data, peeking at the future, and missing costs.
    > "Is there any survivorship bias in your data, and if so, how can you control for it? Have you introduced any look-ahead bias?"
    > "This includes things like execution assumptions and transaction costs."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
17. Hansen's SPA test is a sharper White's Reality Check, and its stepwise versions can pick out a subset of strategies while keeping the overall false-alarm rate under control.
    > "Hansen's test is a Studentized version of White's Reality Check."
    > "The stepwise variants allow one to accept or reject the null of no predictive ability on a subset of some tested strategies while maintaining a familywise error rate."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 10 votes.
18. Aronson's Monte Carlo methods for overfit bias may only suit technical rules.
    > "I believe his techniques for minimizing the bias may only apply to technical strategies, because they rely on Monte Carlo simulations."
    — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 10 votes.
19. Eckhardt's list: a system must pass tests for overfitting, hindsight, odd return shapes, statistical artifacts and one-off circumstances.
    > "tests for (1) overfitting, (2) post-dictiveness, (3) maldistribution of returns, (4) statistical artifacts and (5) the degree to which a system takes advantage of unusual and possibly nonrepeatable circumstances."
    — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), question text (20 votes).
20. The position permutation test asks whether the system's long/short/out choices beat random ones, which is a different question from White's "are returns above zero".
    > "The null in White's RC is that the system's returns are zero"
    > "in the position permutation test the null is that the system's positioning (long, short or out of the market) is no better than random."
    — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), answer 5 votes.
21. There is a ready R package for White's and Hansen's tests.
    > "there is an R package, ttrTests , which implements White's RC and Hansen's SPA"
    — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), answer 5 votes.
22. Meucci's "Prayer" is a ten-step checklist for modelling the profit-and-loss of positions.
    > "a recipe of ten sequential steps for all portfolio managers"
    — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), answer 3 votes.
23. Picking the best of many strategies leaves a trace: its daily results get fatter tails (the asker's idea).
    > "if we generate strategies over and over until one does well, then the distribution of PnLs of the postconditioned strategy has some extra kurtosis."
    — [Here is an approach for measuring Data Snooping; is it new?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/16611), question text (16 votes).
24. That trace is too noisy to see in one year of days, real profits already have fat tails, and a cheater can pick around it.
    > "kurtosis estimated from 252 data points is very noisy"
    > "Real P&Ls already have fat tails"
    > "a sophisticated actor could simply select strategies that happen to have low empirical kurtosis"
    — [Here is an approach for measuring Data Snooping; is it new?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/16611), answer 1 vote.
25. The Probability of Backtest Overfitting (Bailey and others) needs no assumption about the shape of returns: it counts how often the best choice on the training part flops on the test part, over many splits; Harvey and Liu built a multiple-testing framework for strategies.
    > "Bailey's PBO framework is distribution-free and directly applicable to strategy selection problems. It measures what fraction of combinatorial train/test splits result in overfitting."
    > "a multiple testing framework specifically designed for financial strategies"
    — [Here is an approach for measuring Data Snooping; is it new?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/16611), answer 1 vote.
26. Averaging many re-fitted portfolios does not remove the error in the inputs, because every re-fit starts from the same estimates.
    > "there is no theoretical basis for re-sampling."
    > "Averaging will not remove this bias and so all portfolio will inherit this estimation noise."
    — [Comparing MVO with Resampled Efficient Frontier](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/4208), answer 8 votes.
27. Research from a company about its own product deserves doubt.
    > "you must always be skeptical of company research that makes their product look better than their competitors'."
    — [Which approach to estimating fundamental factor models is better, cross-sectional (unobservable) factors or time-series (observable) factors?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1914), answer 17 votes (accepted).

### Out-of-sample and walk-forward

1. The aim of any backtest is that it behaves on new data the way it did on old data.
   > "The goal is to have similar behavior out of sample as you have in sample"
   — [What are the popular methodologies to minimize data snooping?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140), answer 19 votes.
2. Ask whether your history covers many kinds of market, or whether you happened to pick a kind one.
   > "Is your historical sample representative of a wide range of market conditions, or did you (happen to) pick a favorable dataset?"
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 43 votes.
3. No theory says how much data to hold back, and the answer can swing with that choice.
   > "there is no scientific theory that suggests what fraction of the data should be assigned to training and testing and results can be very sensitive to these choices."
   — [What is the ideal ratio of in-sample length to out-of-sample length?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480), answer 10 votes (accepted).
4. Chan's rule: keep the newer part for testing, make the two parts about equal, and never let the test part be under a third of the training part.
   > "Save the second (more recent) part of the data for out-of-sample testing."
   > "The two portions should be roughly equal in size, but if there is insufficient training data, we should at least have one-third as much test data as training data."
   — [What is the ideal ratio of in-sample length to out-of-sample length?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480), answer 10 votes (accepted).
5. Held-back data is fresh only once; tuning on it turns it into training data.
   > "If you optimize your strategy "out-of-sample" you will incur data snooping bias via curve fitting nevertheless!"
   > "the Virginal status of the data reserved for out-of-sample testing has a short life span. It is lost as soon as it is used one time."
   — [What is the ideal ratio of in-sample length to out-of-sample length?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480), answer 10 votes (accepted).
6. Try different split sizes across many assets and look at the spread of results, especially the bad tail.
   > "One approach to answer this question would be to test on many different assets and compare the results of different in sample/out of sample ratios."
   > "I would be looking for not only high average Sharpe Ratio but few low outliers."
   — [What is the ideal ratio of in-sample length to out-of-sample length?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480), answer 3 votes.
7. Another way is k-fold or leave-one-out cross-validation over chunks of the series.
   > "Another approach is to use k fold or leave one out cross-validation by splitting the time series into a series of "chunks""
   — [What is the ideal ratio of in-sample length to out-of-sample length?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480), answer 0 votes.
8. Tuning on 80% and checking on 20% still uses the 20% to decide, so it is not clean.
   > "Even if you tune your model parameters (and I'm not talking about any TA scheme) with 80% of the data and then "check" your model with the remaining 20%, you're still using that last 20% to determine if your model is useful."
   — [Is Walk Forward Analysis a good method to estimate the edge of a trading system?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737), answer 7 votes (accepted).
9. Only live trading proves a model, so build checks that drop it at the first sign of trouble; most models get dropped.
   > "the only way to prove its value is to use it in real time."
   > "it is worthwhile to build tests that allow you to dump your model at the first sign of trouble."
   > "From personal experience, I can tell you that you'll dump far more models than you'll keep."
   — [Is Walk Forward Analysis a good method to estimate the edge of a trading system?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737), answer 7 votes (accepted).
10. A walk-forward process from a technical-indicator book is said to reduce the build-test-retest problem.
    > "The approach in the book helps mitigate the problem described above of assemble/test/retest. When you finally implement a trading system, it should have a good chance of success."
    — [Is Walk Forward Analysis a good method to estimate the edge of a trading system?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737), answer 4 votes.
11. One low-voted answer calls walking forward with regular re-tuning the best check for overfitting.
    > "The best way IMO to validate a system is to walk the system forward, periodically re-optimizing. This will quickly tell you if you are over-fitting"
    — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), answer 0 votes.
12. A backtest only shows one stretch of time; a simulation over many made-up histories says more (Michaud's view).
    > "It is important to note that simulation tests are far superior to back tests. A back test proves absolutely nothing. It only tells you what happened during some time period. A different time period may have very different results."
    — [Comparing MVO with Resampled Efficient Frontier](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/4208), answer 5 votes.
13. Overlapping windows (a 180-day return started on each of 1,000 days) are not independent results; counting them as independent is wrong.
    > "Treating them as if they were non-overlapping seems wrong to me (it is wrong)"
    — [Empirical distribution function of overlapping time series data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/17441), question text (10 votes).
14. Summed overlapping returns carry k times the error of single-period returns, so in a clean world overlapping adds nothing; it helps only when it washes out a repeating error.
    > "times the variance of an OLS fit to one-period returns"
    > "If this were the whole story, we would conclude that there's never any real reason to oversample like this, and leave it at that."
    > "then those patterns will be "washed out" in the annual sum."
    — [Empirical distribution function of overlapping time series data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/17441), answer 1 vote (accepted).
15. A paper gives unbiased formulas for mean, volatility and correlation from overlapping observations.
    > "This paper examines parameter estimation (mean, volatility and correlation) for correlated Brownian processes that makes use of overlapping return observations."
    — [Empirical distribution function of overlapping time series data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/17441), answer 1 vote.
16. Use only what was known at the time, and keep delisted names in the test.
    > "Be careful to include stocks that have been delisted in your backtest, and generally only use criteria that would have been known at the time to construct your sample"
    — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), answer 6 votes.
17. Store data as of when you could have acted on it, not only when it happened.
    > "a good data warehouse will be bitemporal or point-in-time"
    — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 12 votes.
18. Lining up two series by row number instead of by time can make you read one series' future when the other is missing bars.
    > "using an integer indexed array would mean I'm looking at future data for the broad index vs present data for the given security."
    — [What is the best data structure/implementation for representing a time series?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/613), question text (33 votes).
19. A "perfect information" result, built from the future data itself, gives an upper bound to compare real methods against.
    > "Calculate weights using future perfect data. Repeat for all time periods."
    — [How to evaluate minimum-variance strategies against perfect information mvp](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/19285), answer 0 votes.
20. An unanswered question: should losing symbols be thrown out before the forward test? (No answer in the pull addressed it.)
    > "Do you normally throw out symbols with negative returns when doing a forward test as part of your portfolio ?"
    — [How to vet an intraday strategy](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2418), question text (9 votes).

### Transaction costs and slippage

1. Do not assume you trade exactly at the closing price for free.
   > "Are you naively assuming, for example, that you can perfectly execute at the day's closing price? Did you remember frictional costs?"
   — [What are the key risks to the quantitative strategy development process?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), answer 43 votes.
2. The "torture test": on the bar after the signal, a long buys at the bar's high and sells at a bar's low (shorts the reverse).
   > "for longs - enter at the bar high and exit at the bar low on bar following signal bar"
   > "I had previously heard this approach to back testing called the "torture test.""
   — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), answer 13 votes.
3. If a system falls apart under the torture test, it depends on getting good entries and exits.
   > "If an otherwise acceptable system seriously degrades when subjected to this test, it shows that the system is highly reliant on getting good entries and/or exits."
   — [Tests that any system must pass to be taken seriously](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), answer 5 votes.
4. The gap between buy and sell prices can be wider than the bar's whole high-low range.
   > "you will also need to correct for the bid/ask spread, which may be wider than the high/low for some stocks at some times."
   — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), answer 6 votes.
5. Never trade more than 5% of normal volume, and on minute bars assume you can only trade the 10th-percentile minute's volume.
   > "a good proxy is to never be trading more than 5% of the typical volume that takes place in a given period."
   > "if you intend to buy $1MM of some stock over, say, a half hour period, be sure that at least 20MM is typically traded during that half hour of the day."
   > "assume you will not be able to trade more than the 10th percentile of the distribution."
   — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), answer 6 votes.
6. Compare your own backtester against a bought, well-used one to find your bugs.
   > "At the very least, you have a production implementation (with thousands of professional man-hours of effort) to compare your own custom software results and debug issues that surface."
   — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), answer 4 votes.
7. No single cost percentage is realistic; it depends on the asset, the period and the time of day, and the spread is the price of liquidity rather than a fee.
   > "We cannot give you a relative bid-ask spread that would make sense."
   > "The period during which you're trading (I think the liquidity in markets hasn't been the same over time)."
   > "bid-ask spreads are not transaction cost per-say; they are the market price of liquidity in a sense."
   — [How to account for transaction costs in a simulated market environment?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573), answer 9 votes (accepted).
8. The asker had read 0.2% as the cost for Dow stocks.
   > "read that it might be around 0.2% in this paper"
   — [How to account for transaction costs in a simulated market environment?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573), question text (16 votes).
9. Fill as badly as the bar allows: buy at the minute's high, sell at its low, because live trading brings its own surprises.
   > "So I set my Open price at the HIGHEST price for the minute, and my close price to the LOWEST price for the minute."
   > "You don't want to go live and find that you were too optimistic in your fills."
   > "simulating should be as conservative as possible."
   — [How to account for transaction costs in a simulated market environment?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573), answer 4 votes.
10. Make slippage a dial and turn it up until the strategy breaks.
    > "you can vary the slippage and determine how much slippage will break your algo."
    — [How to account for transaction costs in a simulated market environment?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573), answer 3 votes.
11. A flat 0.5% charge per trading day was the asker's guess, and the torture test felt too harsh.
    > "I'm simulating friction by lopping a flat percentage (say 0.5%) off my returns for each day that I make a trade."
    > "but that was depressing"
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), question text (15 votes).
12. Slippage has two parts: paying above the middle price to get filled, and the gap between the price you wanted and the one you got.
    > "execution slippage (i.e. cost above mid to get your fill)"
    > "tracking slippage (how much price difference between actual close and your fill price)"
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), answer 11 votes (accepted).
13. Buying into a rise means chasing and paying more, and that is the usual case; buying into a fall fills easily.
    > "If one attempts to buy in upward momentum you will be chasing price and will likely need to enrich your order significantly to get a fill (hence more slippage)."
    > "(this is the most frequent scenario, since if you are buying there is probably reason for others to be as well)"
    > "If one is buying in downward momentum then the fill is incredibly easy to do, as the market is biased towards sellers."
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), answer 11 votes (accepted).
14. Daily bars cannot tell you slippage; use finer data and simulate the order itself.
    > "I don't believe an analytic approach on OHLC will ever work as cannot determine without seeing the microstructure or intraday price action."
    > "Slippage is a function of execution approach and local price action behavior (vol, buyer/seller bias, etc)."
    > "you should obtain higher-frequency data and simulate your signal and execution algo to get an accurate picture."
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), answer 11 votes (accepted).
15. A resting order priced at a guessed close is cheapest but may miss the real close.
    > "The second is optimal in terms of execution costs, but projected target may deviate from actual close."
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), answer 11 votes (accepted).
16. Check which "close" your data holds; the one you can actually trade may differ.
    > "If it contains consolidated prices then you could find that you realize different prices than your backtest."
    — [How to simulate slippage](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264), answer 6 votes.
17. Do not charge a fixed number of basis points per trade; a taker-only strategy is the easiest to backtest.
    > "I do not recommend modeling the transaction costs as a static number of bps"
    > "A strictly liquidity-taking strategy is easier to backtest."
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
18. For a taker strategy, test at the smallest size on offer, go live, then raise size by hand and measure capacity from real profits.
    > "backtest the strategy with a min size that is available at touch, put the strategy into production, then ramp up the size by hand, and calibrating for capacity based on real world PnL."
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
19. The same answer claims a taker's profit can grow with size because it pushes the price its own way (a doubtful claim; see "Where answers disagree").
    > "your PnL should get better with larger size because you're dislocating the spread further in your trade direction."
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
20. For a resting order, count a fill only when the price trades through it; that is safe for slow strategies but wrong for ones that earn from providing liquidity.
    > "assume that the price that your order is on must trade through completely, i.e. a trade must post on the next level, to simulate a fill."
    > "This is imperfect, since it is conservative on the fill probability."
    > "If the alpha that you're capturing is mostly from providing liquidity, then this usually doesn't work since it will underestimate the fills with adverse selection."
    > "and passive orders are only being used to reduce slippage, this still works fairly well for backtesting and in practice."
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
21. With price-level data, assume every new order joins behind yours and every cancel comes from the back; fancier queue guessing is not worth it.
    > "you can just assume that all adds go behind your order while all cancels go to the backmost of a level."
    > "sophistication is not always a good thing"
    > "Some sophisticated trading firms had an edge from modeling queue position on MBP data, but that trade almost instantly vanished after MBO was introduced."
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
22. With order-by-order data the hard parts are the exchange round trip and calibrating to your real fills (the author sells such data).
    > "(i) estimating matching engine round trip times and (ii) calibrating your estimate based on real world fills."
    > "(Disclaimer: The firm I work for, Databento is one of them.)"
    — [Backtesting using microstructure (orderbook) data](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006), answer 11 votes (accepted).
23. An edge of 1 cent a trade before costs is probably eaten by commissions alone.
    > "on close examination I see that I am making about 1 cent per trade (I haven't taken transaction cost into account)."

### Position sizing and Kelly

1. Betting to grow the log of your money makes you richer in the long run almost surely; betting for the highest average return almost surely ruins you, because it puts everything on the best bet every time.
   > "Maximizing the expected logarithm leads to more wealth almost surely in the long run. In contrast, maximizing expected return can easily lead to going broke almost surely in the long run!"
   > "Maximizing expected return results in betting everything on your highest expected return investment. Repeatedly doing that over time typically leads to polarized outcomes: you become fantastically wealthy or (more likely) destitute."
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 25 votes.
2. Growing the log is the same as growing the compound (geometric) rate; if the average log return is below zero you go broke in the long run, and betting all of it each time gives a chance of ending with nothing that climbs toward certainty with each bet.
   > "When you maximize the log return or log wealth, you're maximizing the expected geometric growth rate."
   > "you will go broke almost surely over the long run."
   > "probability of having 0 wealth"
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 25 votes.
3. The Kelly bet comes from maximising the log over a win-or-lose bet; it gives the fraction (p(b+1) - 1) / b, where p is the chance of winning and b the payout per dollar.
   > "Kelly criterion: Maximizing the expectation of the logarithm gives the problem"
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 25 votes.
4. Log is not the only sensible goal, and errors in the inputs make a "growth optimal" bet much riskier than it looks.
   > "Log utility isn't magic. Other specifications, possibly with higher risk aversion, can be justified."
   > "Estimation errors can lead the estimated growth optimal portfolio to be substantially more risky than a naive analysis might suggest."
   > "You can get into extremely dangerous garbage in, garbage out problems when computing some notion of an optimal portfolio based upon highly uncertain inputs."
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 25 votes.
5. One view: the log is a built-in caution that balances average gain against swings; chasing the plain average means unlimited appetite for risk.
   > "is a subtle way of incorporating risk aversion in the utility."
   > "is basically saying that you have linear utility which corresponds to infinite risk appetite"
   > "In practice it can easily lead to ruin almost surely if the game is played long enough although the linear utility function is blind to that."
   > "the second order term ensures that there will be a balance found between expectation and variance."
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 14 votes (accepted).
6. The other view, in Kelly's own words: he used logs only because they add up over repeated bets, and he assumed the odds were known; someone who cannot reinvest should bet for the plain average.
   > "The original paper was concerned with optimizing the long run geometric return."
   > "He also assumes the probabilities are known."
   > "The reason has nothing to do with the value function which he attached to his money, but merely with the fact that it is the logarithm which is additive in repeated bets and to which the law of large number applies."
   > "if the gambler's wife allowed him to bet one dollar each week but not to reinvest his earnings. He should then maximize his expectation (expected value of capital) on each bet."
   — [Why does Kelly maximise $E[\log\space G]$ rather than simply $E[G]$?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), answer 9 votes.
7. The win-or-lose Kelly formula fits trading poorly; the continuous version sets leverage to mean excess return over variance (Sharpe ratio over volatility), and the best compound growth rate is the risk-free rate plus half the Sharpe ratio squared.
   > "That's not so useful in trading, where the outcomes are continuous"
   > "The continuous Kelly criterion states that for every"
   > "What Ernie is talking about is that the maximum compounded growth rate"
   > "We usually drop the risk-free rate (unless we post treasuries for margin)"
   — [Kelly criterion and Sharpe ratio](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197), answer 18 votes (accepted).
8. In the win-or-lose case Kelly never bets more than you have, because a loss takes the whole stake.
   > "The Kelly criterion is designed to protect your equity from "ruin", so it will never tell you to bet more than what you have in the discrete case"
   — [Kelly criterion and Sharpe ratio](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197), answer 18 votes (accepted).
9. Another answer says Kelly and Sharpe only share inputs: one is about size, the other about return per unit of swing.
   > "I would not put too much weight on any relationship between Sharpe ratio and Kelly criterion. The two are simply not logically related other than they both share common inputs."
   > "Kelly relates to sizing your position while Sharpe ratios relate your excess returns to the volatility of those."
   — [Kelly criterion and Sharpe ratio](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197), answer 6 votes.
10. Chan's mean-over-variance sizing assumes independent strategies with bell-curve returns, and looks simpler than it is.
    > "This assumes that the trading strategies are statistically independent and their returns have a normal distribution."
    > "The formula looks deceptively simple."
    — [How to optimally allocate capital among trading strategies?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2129), question text (19 votes).
11. Full Kelly has known pitfalls, and professionals who chase growth add constraints; the textbook formula needs known odds and outcomes.
    > "Chan spends the rest of the chapter discussing the pitfalls of investing at "full Kelly"."
    > "Professionals may maximize geometric growth, but I don't know anyone who does so with such a simple and unconstrained objective function."
    > "The Kelly formula you cite is based on discrete events with known probabilities and outcomes."
    > "the formula from his book is an approximation of the Kelly formula for continuous outcomes."
    — [How to optimally allocate capital among trading strategies?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2129), answer 20 votes (accepted).
12. Sizing is the third layer after a forecast and a trading rule; it is useless without an edge.
    > "I think it is always helpful to separate a trading system into three general levels: Forecast Trading Rule Risk management"
    > "But you must first have some edge (forecast) to use it!"
    — [How to optimally allocate capital among trading strategies?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2129), answer 15 votes.
13. Splitting money across strategies by mean and covariance works only if those inputs are measured well.
    > "Key to this approach is accurate estimation of your the input parameters identified above."
    — [How to optimally allocate capital among trading strategies?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2129), answer 3 votes.
14. The asker claims Kelly is optimal only when returns are well behaved.
    > "the Kelly criterion is only optimal in a nice and well-behaved Merton-world. It is far from optimal when things are getting non-(log)normal (i.e. more realistic!)."
    — [Optimality of Kelly criterion in non-normal environment](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1374), question text (12 votes).
15. The accepted answer: Kelly was built for two-outcome bets and using it for anything else invites trouble.
    > "The Kelly Criterion was derived for two-outcome events (binomial). Assuming it "works" for anything else (including "normal" events) is asking for trouble."
    — [Optimality of Kelly criterion in non-normal environment](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1374), answer 2 votes (accepted).
16. The derivation itself only needs the law of large numbers, plus a finite average log return.
    > "At this point we have made only one assumption, namely that theorem of large number works."
    — [Optimality of Kelly criterion in non-normal environment](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1374), answer 5 votes.
17. For non-normal returns, simulate outcomes, pick the portfolio with the best median, and cap leverage to get a fractional Kelly.
    > "The purpose of the Kelly criterion is to find a betting strategy that maximizes the geometric growth rate."
    > "It would also be trivial to add constraints to the mean-variance problem in case the investor does not wish to have too much leverage (equivalent to a fractional Kelly-like solution)."
    > "when calculating the median return could use a simulation-based set of returns at the horizon."
    — [Optimality of Kelly criterion in non-normal environment](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1374), answer 1 vote.

20. In one simulation of a million runs, sizing as a fixed share of capital gave the worst Sharpe ratio of four rules.

21. Sizing theory splits into growth (Kelly) and mean-variance; Kelly targets growth, not a steady Sharpe, and is unbeatable over an endless horizon only when the odds and payout are known, which in trading they are not.
    > "From what I can tell the two broad branches of position sizing split into capital growth theory and variants of mean-variance."
    > "In the context of Kelly, the Sharpe ratio may not be the best metric since it is trying to maximize growth rather than stable growth as measured by the Sharpe ratio."
    > "It has been shown that if you know the probability of success and the payout then there exists no other algorithm that outperforms the Kelly criterion over an infinitely long horizon (in terms of maximizing growth, not Sharpe)."
    > "Much of the problem is that we, of course, don't have the probability of success upfront"
    — [Forget Kelly, forget fractional sizing. Where is the general theory?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/50630), answer 2 votes.
22. Even a winning system has losing streaks, which push an early Kelly estimate below zero.
    > "Even when you have a system that has positive expectancy, you can (and usually will) sustain a number of losses, sometimes consecutively."

23. A negative Kelly means do not bet; a Kelly number that keeps flipping sign means its assumptions do not hold, so estimate over a longer horizon.
    > "which means you should not bet any of your wealth at this moment if you don't have the ability to take the opposite bet"
    > "if your estimated probabilities are changing sufficiently for the calculated kelly ratio to turn negative and positive at various times, then that means you are violating one of the key assumptions of the strategy."
    > "The strategy only works if you are able to sustain both the edge and the odds (p and b) for the long term, and therefore maximize your long term wealth."
    > "You should aim to estimate probabilities for a longer time horizon for your strategy to make sense."
    > "using such trade signals is likely to lead to large error rates in the odds (b)"
    — [How to apply the Kelly criterion when expected return may be negative?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2500), answer 7 votes.
24. Continuous Kelly assumes no trading costs, and in the asker's simulations it did not always win; it needs accurate inputs and re-sizing at every rebalance.

    > "This output can then be passed to a bet sizing algorithm which maps the output to a position size. The core idea being that we want to take large positions on trades that are likely to be true and smaller positions on trades when we are unsure."

30. With borrowed money, a loss past your margin ends the game even if the trade would have come back.
    > "If your mark-to-market current loss exceeds your current margin requirements and you don't have a liquidity buffer, the game is over even if your sold options are still OTM"
    > "In short, if you systematically sell short-term Gamma to harvest some variance risk premium, you end up... dead."
    — [Risk management tools for long term Gamma/Vega sellers subject to margin calls](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/41695), question text (15 votes).
31. Size leveraged trades by stress tests, keep spare cash, and avoid piling up around big news; most blow-ups are a cash squeeze, not a bad average.
    > "The main risk is not just P&L drawdown, but margin expansion during stress regimes."
    > "Size the trade so that worst-case stress does not exhaust available liquidity."
    > "Many gamma sellers fail not because of long-run expectation but because of short-term liquidity squeeze. A rule of thumb: Hold at least 2x the maximum simulated margin expansion."
    > "Avoid clustering around event risk (earnings, macro events)"
    > "Margin stress causes forced liquidation at worst times."
    > "Survival depends on conservative sizing, stress simulation, and maintaining excess capital buffers."
    — [Risk management tools for long term Gamma/Vega sellers subject to margin calls](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/41695), answer 1 vote (accepted).
32. Use dollars, not percentages, when lot sizes, position limits or tick sizes matter, because those limits cannot be written as returns.

### Stops

1. A study found that stops reduce how much a position swings but do not raise its expected return; stops and profit-takes, alone or together, made no significant difference, and when prices drift the result just converges to holding the asset.
   > "Has it been shown to work through proper statistical backtests?"
   — [Any research paper on stop loss?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2785), question text (10 votes).
   > "The use of stop-losses change return distributions, but not in the way that one would typically expect."
   > "We find that while stop-losses can reduce position volatility, hidden costs offset perceived benefits in terms of altering future returns."
   > "Use of both stop-losses and profit-taking stops separately or in conjunction offer no statistically significant difference in expected return but have a meaningful impact in returns with drift, as the expected return converges to that of the underlying."
   — [Any research paper on stop loss?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2785), answer 11 votes.
2. Buying when the price rises through a level and selling when it falls back through it looks free, but it is not: the cost builds up from the price wiggling around that level (from one of the paper's authors).
   > "The strategy seems to be self financing because the investor's only actions are to buy stock in the market when the stock price increases to the exercise price K, simultaneously borrowing K dollars, or to liquidate the stock in the market when it decreases to K, simultaneously repaying the K dollar loan."
   — [Stop-loss start-gain paradox: Why is it a 'paradox'?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/38582), answer 4 votes.
   > "I am one of the two authors of the paper."
   > "In fact, if the underlying random process had continuous sample paths of bounded variation, then the binary trading strategy is actually self-financing. In contrast, when these continuous sample paths have unbounded variation, then the strategy is not self financing, but the failure occurs only around the strike price."
   > "Roughly speaking this local time is the accumulation of squared underlying price changes experienced around the strike."
   > "This synthesis was the theoretical basis for the construction of VIX."
   — [Stop-loss start-gain paradox: Why is it a 'paradox'?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/38582), answer 15 votes.
3. In a simple backtester a stop fills at the next bar's low after the trigger; the torture test is harsher still.
   > "I would also simulate stop-loss sell orders and they, too, would be executed at the next bar low after the price passed the threshold."
   — [How to design a custom equity backtester?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), question text (28 votes).
4. Limit and stop orders may never fill, and most close-to-close tools do not model them; the asker wanted bracket orders (one target, one stop, one cancels the other).
   > "A lot of the tools/libraries out there seem to focus on close to close time series analysis."
   > "I will use limit or stop orders that may or may not get filled."
   > "It would also be good if I could put OCO bracket orders for profit/loss stops."
   — [What tools and libraries may be used to model limit/stop systematic trading?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2554), question text (12 votes).
   > "The quantstrat R package (which seems to be a part of TradeAnalytics) might be a good solution"
   — [What tools and libraries may be used to model limit/stop systematic trading?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2554), answer 1 vote.
5. Taleb said comparing the high-low range with close-to-close swings shows mean reversion and how stops get hit; a reader could not reproduce it, and the answer says Taleb made an arithmetic slip: the ratio should be 1, not 1.66.
   > "Comparing the Parkinson number and the periodically sampled volatility helps traders understand the mean reversion in the market as well as the distribution of stop-losses."
   > "Additional testing by the author shows the bias to be permanent in close to the 20 markets surveyed."
   > "seems a cap rather than a floor"
   — [Does your Parkinson volatility ratio work as Taleb explained?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43794), question text (16 votes).
   > "I believe that Taleb made a mistake in his book."
   > "I believe that the theoretical ratio of Parkinson number to close-to-close volatility should be 1, instead of 1.66"
   > "if you replace close and open prices with high and low prices to calculate volatility, then that vol value would be 1.66 times of true vol in ideal markets."
   — [Does your Parkinson volatility ratio work as Taleb explained?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43794), answer 9 votes (accepted).
6. With a target and stop of equal size the win rate sits near one half, so most of the time the right move is not to trade.
   > "For reasonably and equally sized target and stop, the feasible win rate will be close to"
   > "Most of times the outcome is not predictable and the right choice is not to open a position."
   > "the widely used binary cross-entropy loss strongly penalizes confident mis-classifications weakly rewards being rightfully more confident and is not necessarily appropriate here."
   — [Defining an objective function for machine learning task of trading](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/44164), question text (9 votes).
7. Choose when to trade by expected profit after cost, not by accuracy: the example wins 80, loses 100 and pays 4 per trade.
   > "Adjusting the threshold"
   > "EV[p_] := p*80 - (1 - p)*100 - 4"
   — [Defining an objective function for machine learning task of trading](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/44164), answer 7 votes (accepted).

### Sharpe ratio significance

No question in the pull asks directly how to test whether a Sharpe ratio is real. These are the lessons that touch it.

1. The Sharpe ratio is the average excess return divided by its standard deviation.
   > "is given by the ratio of the mean excess return"
   — [Kelly criterion and Sharpe ratio](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197), answer 18 votes (accepted).
2. A rule of thumb for "is this signal real": a t-statistic above 2.
   > "there is one metric that dominates all of them and that is simply the t-stat of the factor."
   > "The rule of the thumb here is to use a t-stat greater than 2."
   — [Variable Selection in factor models](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1369), answer 1 vote.
3. Also check how often the signal has the right sign and how fast its link to returns fades.
   > "Fraction same sign / Hit rate"
   > "correlation coefficient decay over lagged periods can prove insightful in selecting factors with higher stability and persistence."
   — [Variable Selection in factor models](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1369), answer 4 votes (accepted).
4. Judge a backtest on many numbers, not return alone, and remember the starting portfolio changes what "good" means.
   > "We also don't look just at returns but many different metrics including but not limited to, max drawdown, variance of draw downs, number of trades, holding period, correlation (or lack of) with various indexes, Sharpe ratio, commissions paid, share turnover, and numerous other measures of variance."
   — [How to properly evaluate backtest returns?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2516), answer 6 votes.
   > "shows an example of how the results from a backtest can be deceiving."
   > "The main issue is that the portfolio you start with can have an impact on what "good" means."
   — [How to properly evaluate backtest returns?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2516), answer 6 votes.
5. Sharpe and profit per share are fine working measures while building a model.
   > "In any case Sharpe and profit p/share are good metrics for evaluation."
   — [How to vet an intraday strategy](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2418), answer 1 vote.
6. A hedging or trading frequency can be chosen to give the best Sharpe ratio after costs.
   > "You can formulate the problem of finding the optimal hedging frequency under transaction costs to maximize the Sharpe ratio of the delta-hedging strategy."
   — [Delta hedging frequency for plain vanilla European options under trading costs](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/10623), answer 3 votes.
7. If returns have very fat tails, averages and spreads may not exist at all and the sample average is no better than one observation, so mean-and-squares tools (Sharpe among them) can mislead; median-based (quantile) methods hold up.
   > "all moments are undefined, the sample mean is distributed exactly the same as a single observation"
   — [Suppose that we are wrong about the relevant class of distributions for financial economics and econometrics. Now what?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/52960), question text (10 votes).
   > "any squares minimizing tool will generate potentially spurious results."
   > "Quantile regression produces an unbiased estimator"
   > "The very first test in the literature by Osborne showed the method to be very successful, but they had to trim the data because the tails were too broad."
   — [Suppose that we are wrong about the relevant class of distributions for financial economics and econometrics. Now what?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/52960), answer 9 votes (accepted).
8. Entropy is a risk measure that still works when tails are too fat for a standard deviation.
   > "Fortunatelly the entropy is defined for fat-tailed distribution."
   > "Higher the entropy, higher the uncertainty and the risk."
   — [Suppose that we are wrong about the relevant class of distributions for financial economics and econometrics. Now what?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/52960), answer 2 votes.
9. Pooling information across related cases predicts better than each case alone: in Efron's baseball example the pooled estimate had 2/7 of the error.
   > "Effron tries to predict the final batting average of a group of major league players based on the first forty-five at-bats."
   > "It implies that there is information about the parameters of all batters in the batting history of all the others."
   > "The Bayesian estimator had 2/7ths of the variance of the prediction versus the final batting average."
   — [Suppose that we are wrong about the relevant class of distributions for financial economics and econometrics. Now what?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/52960), answer 9 votes (accepted).
10. Normality tests need independent samples; cumulative returns from one path are not independent, the step-by-step returns are.
    > "The test I am using to test for normality (Anderson-Darling) relies on independent samples from a (supposed) normal distribution, and as a couple people have pointed out in the comments"
    — [Distribution of Geometric Brownian Motion](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/10516), question text (12 votes).
11. Intraday data adds statistical power to a short history (in crypto, history before 2017 is judged unrepresentative).
    > "We only deem return data from 2017 representative, but the use of intraday data boosts statistical power."
    — [Something fundamentally different about cryptocurrencies?](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/36342), answer 7 votes.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** The answers agree that costs decide whether an idea is real, and that one flat percentage is the wrong way to charge them: the spread changes by asset, period and time of day, buying into a rise costs more than buying into a fall, and a resting order is only safe to count as filled when the price trades through it. The practical advice is to charge pessimistic fills (the "torture test"), make slippage a dial and turn it until the strategy breaks, and correct the cost model from real fills. An edge of a cent a trade before costs is called not viable. Impact grows roughly with the square root of size, but every number for it is disputed and hard to measure.

**Overfitting.** Trying many ideas on the same data guarantees that some look good by luck. The fixes named are fewer settings, settings that do not flip results when nudged, ideas with a reason behind them that work on many assets, and statistical corrections for the number of tests (Benjamini-Hochberg, White's Reality Check, Hansen's SPA, Romano-Wolf, Bailey's Probability of Backtest Overfitting), plus tests against random positions. The strongest advice is about the tester: decide what you expect before you look at the money, and stop tweaking.

**Testing on unseen data.** Keep the newest data back, at least a third the size of the training part, and treat it as spent the moment you use it to choose; no theory fixes the split, so try several. Walk-forward still uses the checking window to decide, so the only final proof is live trading, with a rule that drops a model at the first sign of trouble. Overlapping windows are not independent results.

**Position size.** Kelly (bet to grow the log of your money) beats betting for the highest average in the long run, but only if the odds and payout are known, and in trading they are estimated; errors in them make full Kelly dangerous. Professionals add constraints or bet a fraction; others warn against Kelly entirely. If the Kelly number keeps changing sign, its assumptions are broken and the answer is to not bet. Sizing needs an edge first.

**Leverage.** The continuous Kelly formula gives leverage as Sharpe over volatility, but the practical warning comes from option sellers: with borrowed money a loss past the margin ends the game even if the trade would recover, so size by stress tests, keep spare cash (twice the worst simulated margin increase), and stay away from big news.

**Stops.** The one study cited found stops cut swings without raising the expected return, and target-plus-stop rules made no significant difference; a stop-and-re-enter at one level looks free but pays for every wiggle around that level. With a target and stop of equal size the win rate sits near half, so most of the time the right choice is not to trade, and the decision to trade should be made on expected profit after costs.

## Claims we could test on our own data

This repo measures millions of settings each night on Gate's crypto futures, chooses on older days and judges on unseen days beside random entries, charges a 0.075% fee each way plus each minute's recorded order book and funding, and trades $5 margin at 20x. These claims from the pull could be checked there:

1. **Many tests make lucky winners** ([q/140](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140)). Count how many settings beat their random-entry bar on unseen days, then apply Benjamini-Hochberg (or Romano-Wolf / Hansen's SPA) to those comparisons and see how many survive. If the survivors are about the number luck predicts, the claim is confirmed for this store.
2. **Positions no better than random** ([q/3225](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225)). For a chosen setting, shuffle its long/short/out positions in time many times and see where its real unseen profit falls among the shuffles.
3. **Probability of Backtest Overfitting** ([q/16611](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/16611)). Split each coin-timeframe's history into many blocks, choose the best setting on half of them and score it on the other half, over every combination; the share where the chosen setting lands below the middle is the overfitting probability.
4. **Picked winners have fatter tails** ([q/16611](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/16611), the asker's idea). Compare the kurtosis of per-trade profits for rows chosen as best against random rows of the same coin-timeframe. The answer says 252 points is too few; this store has far more trades, so the test is possible here.
5. **Real profits are a fraction of simulated ones** ([q/147](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147)). Compare each practice room's closed trades with the backtest's trades for the same strategies and days, and report the ratio of profits.
6. **Held-back data is spent once used** ([q/1480](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480)). When a nightly picker re-chooses using the same recent days it is judged on, compare its next-month result with a picker that never looks at the judging days.
7. **The split size changes the answer** ([q/1480](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1480)). Re-run the older/unseen choice at 50/50, 70/30 and 75/25 across all coins and compare the spread of results and the bad tail, not only the average.
8. **Walk-forward with re-tuning** ([q/3225](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225), [q/737](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737)). Re-choose a setting every month on the months before it and add up only the next-month results.
9. **The torture test** ([q/839](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), [q/3225](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225)). Re-price stored winners so a long buys at the next bar's high and sells at a bar's low; a winner that collapses depends on perfect fills.
10. **Find the slippage that breaks a strategy** ([q/3573](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573), [q/2418](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2418)). Re-run winners at 1x, 2x and 3x their recorded spread cost and find where profit reaches zero.
11. **Buying into a rise costs more** ([q/1264](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264)). From the recorded minute books, compare the price a $100 buy really gets after an up-candle with after a down-candle (and the reverse for sells).
12. **Providing liquidity can make slippage negative** ([q/284](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/284)). Compare waiting (post-only) entries with market entries on the same signals: fill rate, price got, and what the missed trades would have made.
13. **Trade-through fills are conservative** ([q/70006](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006)). Count waiting orders the price only touched against those it traded through, and what the touched-only trades made; the answer warns that for liquidity-providing strategies this rule undercounts the fills that go wrong.
14. **Stops do not change expected return** ([q/2785](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2785)). Take the same entries, exit after a fixed number of bars with no stop or target, and compare the average profit per trade before costs with the stop-and-target versions, separately in trending and flat months.
15. **Equal stop and target win about half the time** ([q/44164](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/44164)). Check the before-cost win rate of equal-barrier rows; with the answer's example numbers (win 80, lose 100, cost 4) break-even is a 57.8% win rate (my arithmetic from its formula).
16. **The high-low to close-to-close volatility ratio** ([q/43794](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43794)). Compute it per coin; the answer says it should be 1. Then test Taleb's claim that a higher ratio marks mean-reverting coins by comparing mean-reversion strategy results above and below the median ratio.
17. **Crypto follows its own trend** ([q/36342](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/36342)). The time-series momentum claim can be judged on the unseen days, after every cost, against random entries; volatility targeting can be tested as a sizing rule on the same trades.
18. **Correlation rises in crashes** ([q/36342](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/36342)). Measure each coin's correlation with BTC on BTC's worst 5% of days against all other days.
19. **A Kelly number that flips sign means no edge** ([q/2500](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2500)). Work out each strategy's Kelly fraction from its win rate and average win and loss after costs on the older days, then on the unseen days, and count how often the sign flips.

## Where answers disagree

1. **Why Kelly uses the log.** The accepted answer says the log builds in caution ("is a subtle way of incorporating risk aversion in the utility.", [q/43369](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/43369), 14 votes); another quotes Kelly himself ("The reason has nothing to do with the value function which he attached to his money", 9 votes); a third says Kelly is "precisely equivalent to assuming logarithmic utility" ([q/39649](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/39649), 2 votes).
2. **Is Kelly only for win-or-lose bets?** The accepted answer in [q/1374](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1374) (2 votes) says using it beyond two outcomes "is asking for trouble"; the accepted answers in [q/7197](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197) (18 votes) and [q/2129](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2129) (20 votes) use a continuous Kelly for trading, and [q/39649](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/39649) (1 vote) says "Kelly can deal with any distribution, not necessarily binary outcomes."
3. **Kelly and Sharpe.** [q/7197](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/7197)'s accepted answer (18 votes) ties them together (leverage = Sharpe over volatility, growth = Sharpe squared over two); the next answer (6 votes) says "I would not put too much weight on any relationship between Sharpe ratio and Kelly criterion."
4. **Use Kelly at all?** Van Tharp "specifically warns against using both the Kelly Criterion and Optimal f" ([q/1318](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1318), 11 votes), while [q/50630](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/50630) (2 votes) says no algorithm beats it over an endless horizon when the odds are known.
5. **Walk-forward.** A 0-vote answer calls walking forward with re-tuning "The best way IMO to validate a system" ([q/3225](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3225)) and a 4-vote answer says a book's method gives "a good chance of success" ([q/737](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737)); the accepted 7-vote answer in [q/737](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/737) says the checking window is still used to decide and only live trading proves value.
6. **What a backtest is worth.** Michaud's 5-vote answer says "A back test proves absolutely nothing." ([q/4208](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/4208)); the highest-voted answers elsewhere ([q/147](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/147), [q/140](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/140)) treat a careful backtest as necessary, just not sufficient.
7. **Resampled portfolios.** One answer (8 votes) says "there is no theoretical basis for re-sampling."; Michaud, who holds the patent, says (5 votes) it is "the only portfolio optimizer with a rigorous mathematical proof of investment effectiveness in the world today." ([q/4208](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/4208)).
8. **How harsh fills should be.** The torture test and "simulating should be as conservative as possible." ([q/839](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/839), [q/3573](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/3573)) against the accepted slippage answer, which says bar data cannot answer it and finer data is needed ([q/1264](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/1264)), and the order-book answer, which warns a conservative fill rule "will underestimate the fills with adverse selection" for liquidity-providing strategies ([q/70006](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006)).
9. **Does size help or hurt?** [q/70006](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/70006) (11 votes) says a taker's "PnL should get better with larger size"; [q/284](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/284) (9 votes) says for a momentum-follower "slippage increases as trade size increases."
10. **One cent a trade.** Not viable ([q/2418](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/2418), accepted, 2 votes) against "actually a good return" for very high-volume market makers (1 vote). They are about different traders, so both may be right.
11. **Crypto as a diversifier.** Within one answer, one paper says crypto "may offer diversification benefits for investors with short investment horizons" and another that it does "not offer protection for investors with short horizons"; one calls it "somewhat illiquid", another credits it with "strong market liquidity" ([q/36342](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/36342), 7 votes).
12. **Rebalancing profit.** "The examples that demonstrate the effect are typically contrived" (15 votes) against a paper saying volatility can be put "to work in a controlled manner" (8 votes) ([q/38473](https://quant.stackexchange.com/q/38473)).
