## What it is

This is the Wikipedia article on mathematical finance, the use of maths and statistics to model money and markets. It splits the field into two halves: pricing derivatives (the "Q world", built on a made-up "risk-neutral" probability) and managing risk and portfolios (the "P world", built on real-world probability). It gives a short history from Louis Bachelier's 1900 thesis through Black, Scholes and Merton, Markowitz and Sharpe, and ends with a criticism section on fat tails, the 2008 crisis and the Lucas critique. It is a reference page, not a how-to: it never explains how to build, test or size a trading strategy, and most trading topics (costs, overfitting, stops, leverage) are not covered.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. Price changes have fatter tails than a bell curve (lessons 47 and 49). Test: for each coin and timeframe in the candle store (15m, 30m, 1h, 4h, 1d), take log returns, measure their spread, and count how many moves lie beyond 3, 4 and 5 times that spread; compare with what a bell curve predicts (and report excess kurtosis). The claim is confirmed if big moves are clearly more common than the bell curve says.

2. Swings grow with the time window to a power a bit above 1/2 (lesson 48). Test: for each coin, compute the spread of log returns at 15m, 30m, 1h, 4h and 1d from the same stretch of candles, fit a straight line of log(spread) against log(window length), and read the slope. The text says a bit more than 0.5; a slope near 0.5 or below would not support it for crypto.

3. Trends say something about the near future (lesson 39, technical analysis's tenet). Test: does the sign of the last N candles' return predict the sign of the next candle's return better than chance, per coin and timeframe, before and after the 0.075% fee each way? This repo's own earlier walk-forward research (CLAUDE.md, Oct 20-22, 2026, not this source) found picks no better than random entries before fees, which already leans against a usable edge.

4. A pattern that paid in the past may not keep paying (lesson 51, the Lucas critique), and estimates from past data are not exact (lesson 37). Test: pick strategies on older days and measure them on later unseen days; the claim holds if profit and win rate shrink a lot from the picking days to the unseen days.

5. Bachelier's rule that short-term changes add up to a bell curve over longer periods (lesson 20). Test: compare the tail counts from claim 1 across timeframes; if the 1d returns are much closer to a bell curve than the 15m returns, that supports it; if fat tails remain at 1d, the text's own criticism (lesson 46) wins.

The 2007 review's 56/20/19 split is a count of other people's studies and cannot be checked on our data.
