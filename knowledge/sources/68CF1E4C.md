## What it is

The "start here" page of Rob Carver's blog. The top tells who he is: 7 years at the hedge fund AHL, now trading his own
money with a system he wrote himself, which trades over 100 futures markets and holds positions for several weeks. Most
of the page is a list of his posts (166 links, 147 different addresses once repeats are counted once), sorted under 22 headings, and a few of those titles say plainly
whether the idea in them worked or failed. Below the list is a comment thread where readers ask him questions; his
answers are the real teaching on this page, especially one long answer (Jun 30, 2017) about why a leveraged backtest
with a tight stop looks far better than it will trade.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** Costs decide how fast you may trade. Spread bets cost about 10 times futures, so their wider buy-sell gap means trading more slowly, and a tax break cannot rescue them. A tight stop trades almost every day, so costs must be "spot on", and the number to know is what share of the account goes on costs each year. A small daily holding fee "has a habit of adding up over a year". Turnover, how much you trade against your average position, is the measure of trading speed, not the number of days you trade. The list has posts on this ("Optimising with costs, and a summary of the gold standard model", "The relationship between instrument cost and momentum performance", "Do fast trading rules actually cost more to trade?"); I did not read them.

**Overfitting.** A great result on one market may be luck, and trying several stop sizes is "implicit fitting" that makes the Sharpe ratio look higher than it is. A Sharpe rise from .82 to .95 after a change "isn't a statistically significant" gain. He distrusts a score built on the single worst drop, because one event is not a robust statistic. The 2026 post series marks four fitting methods as failures, and "The three kinds of overfitting" is listed but not read.

**Testing on unseen data.** The "pure test": many markets, many versions of the rule and of the stop, choose among them "out of sample" (on data not used to pick), realistic prices, slow fills, and expect "a much lower SR". He also warns that the past can flatter the future for reasons outside the system (lower stock and bill returns ahead). The list has a group of posts on testing with random data; not read.

**Position size.** He sizes by risk: a 25% yearly risk target; portfolio risk from weights and the covariance table; a risk measure that reads higher (like one built from highs and lows) needs a lower risk target to match; the signal is scaled by the risk of price changes. Small accounts get their own posts (spread bets help small sizes; choosing a fixed market list "works"). Position inertia is pointed to, with no number of his own.

**Leverage.** Leverage on a strategy whose real Sharpe is lower than its backtest is "extremely dangerous". A strategy with low average risk but high peak risk is "toxic with leverage", and 10x is "crazy". He runs much less leverage than full Kelly would allow. His stress check assumes all markets move together.

**Stops.** A 1% stop means near-daily trading, needs intraday data, and must be tested with fills an hour to several days late. In a crash a stop fills at the day's low: a 4% stop in October 1987 would have filled about 22% down. The post "What is the right way to set stop losses" is listed but not read.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. **A good result on one coin is likely luck** (lessons 31 and 38). Take the best strategy per coin chosen on older days and see how many stay profitable on newer days, against random entries, and against the same rule averaged across all coins. Refuted if per-coin winners keep winning more than random ones.
2. **Picking the best of several stop sizes overstates the result** (lesson 32). For each signal, choose the best stop on older days and compare its newer-day profit with the average of all stop sizes. Confirmed if the chosen stop's lead shrinks or vanishes on the newer days.
3. **A 1% stop means near-daily trading and costs decide it** (lessons 36 and 37). Group stored trades by stop size and measure holding time and cost as a share of the gross move. Confirmed if the tight-stop groups hold for hours and pay a much larger share in costs.
4. **Late fills change the result** (lesson 36). Re-run the same strategies with each entry and exit moved one minute, one candle and one hour later. Confirmed if profit falls a lot for tight stops and little for wide ones.
5. **A stop does not fill at the stop in a jump** (lesson 40). In the minute-by-minute exits, count stop exits where the price opened past the stop and measure how far past. Confirmed if those overshoots exist and are large on the fastest moves; at 20x leverage, check how often one minute moves past the liquidation distance.
6. **Know costs as a yearly share of the account** (lesson 37). For each practice room, add every fee, buy-sell gap and funding paid over a year (or scaled from the days held) and divide by the room's money. A number, not a yes/no; compare it with each room's profit before costs.
7. **Small holding fees add up** (lesson 60). Sum funding paid per strategy over its trades and compare it with the profit. Confirmed if funding is a large share for strategies that hold long.
8. **Removing short trades can raise the score without proof** (lessons 51). Run strategies buy-only and both ways, and test whether the difference in Sharpe is bigger than chance (for example by bootstrap over days).
9. **The worst drop is a weak measure** (lesson 47). Split each strategy's history in two halves and compare how well the worst drop and the Sharpe ratio of the first half predict the second. Confirmed if the worst drop carries over worse.
10. **A high-low risk measure reads higher but predicts risk a bit better** (lessons 43 and 44). On coin candles, compare close-to-close risk with a high-low risk, each as a forecast of the next period's realised risk.
11. **Correlations rise in stress** (lesson 50). Measure how coins move together on the most volatile days against normal days. Confirmed if they move more together on wild days.
12. **Skew changes with daily against monthly returns** (lesson 66). Measure the skew of strategy returns both ways.
13. **Update as often as you hold** (lesson 26). Compare typical holding times per timeframe with how often the rooms check; a strategy held for days gains nothing from being checked every minute, apart from exits.
