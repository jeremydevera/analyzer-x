## What it is

These are the 78 slides of a 2013 workshop (the deck says "this version: 2013-05-22") by Jan Humme and Brian G. Peterson, two of the authors of quantstrat, a free R package for testing trading strategies on past prices. The deck sets out a "scientific" way to build strategies (make a guess, build a model, test it), shows how quantstrat splits a strategy into indicators, signals and rules, and walks through one example, the "Luxor" strategy: a 10-candle and 30-candle average crossing on 30-minute GBPUSD candles from Oct 2002 to Jul 2008, entered with a stop order just past the high or low of the crossing candle. It then shows how to search the settings, add a stop and a target as linked orders, study each trade's worst and best move, and run a walk-forward test (3 months to choose the settings, the next 1 month to test them). Every RESULT of those tests (profit, drawdown, the best settings, the walk-forward curve) was a picture, so the saved text holds the method, the code and one table of numbers (the trade quantiles on slide 62), but not how well Luxor did.

## Everything it teaches

Slides 1-3: why test at all

1. Treat backtesting as science: make a guess, work out what the guess implies, then keep or throw out the guess by experiment.
> "science was the process of first making a guess, then computing the consequences of that guess (building a model), and finally confirming or rejecting the guess with experiment"
(slide 3)

2. The aim of the whole workshop is a way of working where every strategy starts as a hypothesis to be tested.
> "create a scientific, hypothesis-driven approach to quantitative strategy development"
(slide 3)

Slides 4-6: the idea funnel and improving a strategy

3. Write the idea down as a hypothesis first, then test it on past data in a research tool.
> "formulate your hypothesis"
> "test idea in research platform (R, historical data (e.g. Reuters))"
(slide 4)

4. Keep working on an idea only if it looks promising; new ideas often turn up while you test.
> "refine if promising"
> "(or new ideas suggest themselves during testing)"
(slide 4)

5. Throw bad ideas away fast, because almost all ideas fail.
> "reject quickly if not promising"
> "Vast majority (90%+) of ideas will not work out"
(slide 4)

6. An idea that survives must still pass a check that its settings are not fragile, and a walk-forward test.
> "perform parameter robustness and walk forward optimization testing"
(slide 4)

7. Only a believable research model goes to real trading, and the research code IS that model.
> "when you have a credible research model (your quantstrat code), implement for production rollout"
(slide 4)

8. Trying the strategy on new markets can happen both before and after it goes live.
> "product testing may be done both before and after production rollout"
(slide 5)

9. You should be able to say why the strategy makes money.
> "identify hypotheses about the mechanism of action of the strategy. How does your strategy make money?"
(slide 5)

10. Once you know why it works, list other markets where the same reason should hold, and test them.
> "generate lists of instruments that may be subject to the same method of action"
(slide 5)

11. On a new market start from your usual settings, and run the robustness and walk-forward tests only on the markets that look promising.
> "start with your 'standard' parameters or parameter ranges"
> "Walk Forward Optimization on promising assets"
(slide 5)

12. A strategy is never finished while it is still running.
> "no strategy is 'done' until you turn it off"
(slide 6)

13. An improvement idea is a hypothesis too: decide the test, and what counts as success, before you run it.
> "specify tests that will demonstrate improvement based on the hypothesis"
> "specify success criteria for measuring results"
(slide 6)

14. Put the change into the research version of the strategy and re-run the usual setting searches on the markets it already trades.
> "code improvement hypothesis into the reference research implementation of the strategy"
> "perform standard optimizations on instruments currently traded on the strategy"
(slide 6)

15. Ship the change only if results on data it was not tuned on get better.
> "if out of sample performance improves, code changes to put into production"
(slide 6)

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** The deck lists "Evaluate trading costs" and "Estimate pre-trade pricing" as steps of a trade simulation (slide 8), but never says how big a cost to charge. In the Luxor code the fee is a variable (".txn.fees" on slide 28, ".txnfees" on slide 51) whose value never appears in the text; the fee is put on the exit order only, also as an "end of trade marker" (slide 28), and the position-cap example sets the entry fee to 0 (slide 53). The gap between buy and sell prices and slippage are not discussed in words: entries are stop orders priced a "threshold" past the candle's high or low (slides 26-27), and a market order fills on the next bar (slide 26). Funding is not covered.

**Overfitting.** More free settings need more data and raise the chance of overfitting (slide 39); even a first guess at settings is already a choice of the best (slide 39); a good setting sits on a smooth surface with no big peaks that vanish with small changes (slide 45); maximising profit on the choosing months picked poor settings (slide 67); and the conclusion says "overfitting remains a danger" (slide 73). The deck gives no number for how much a result shrinks from choosing days to unseen days.

**Testing on unseen data.** A change goes live only if out-of-sample results improve (slide 6); robustness and walk-forward tests come before going live (slides 4 and 5). Walk forward re-picks settings every so often, rolling or anchored (slide 64); the example chooses on 3 months and tests on 1 (slide 65). The walk-forward result is shown only as pictures (slides 66 and 67). A random-trades check is listed as future work (slide 72), and a Monte Carlo check is suggested on the trade list (slide 36), but no random-entry comparison is run in the deck.

**Position size.** The example order size is a fixed 100,000 (slide 26). Sizing is a swappable function: the default does nothing, and the included one caps the position at set levels, judged by the position held (slides 52-53). Rebalancing rules named "rulePctEquity" and "ruleWeight" exist for sharing money among markets (slide 71), and returns depend on the starting money assumed (slide 70). There is no rule for how much of an account to risk per trade.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. **"90%+ of ideas will not work out" (slide 4).** Count, across every signal family this repo has measured, how many make money after the 0.075% fee each way, each minute's recorded order book and funding, on days they were not chosen on, AND beat random entries with the same number of trades. The share that fails is the test.

2. **Luxor itself (slides 13, 27, 28).** A 10/30 simple-average cross on 30-minute candles, entry by a stop order a small amount past the crossing candle's high (buy) or low (sell), whole position closed at market on the opposite cross. Run it on every Gate coin with the repo's full costs and compare it with random entries. Our engine fills at the next minute's book; it would need a "fills only if the price trades through the stop price" rule for the stop entry, and should count stop entries that never fill.

3. **Smooth settings beat peaky ones (slide 45).** For each coin and timeframe, on the choosing days, pick (a) the single best fast/slow pair, and (b) the pair whose neighbours (one or two steps away) also did well. Judge both on the unseen days beside random entries. If (b) holds up better than (a), the slide's claim holds on crypto.

4. **Choosing the highest profit in walk forward picks poor settings (slide 67).** Roll 3 months to choose, 1 month to test, over Gate history, with "highest profit on the choosing months" as the choice, against other choices (smoothest neighbourhood, smallest drop, or a fixed default). Compare the test months with each other and with random entries.

5. **Rolling against anchored windows (slide 64).** Run the same walk forward with only the latest 3 months, and with all months from the start, and compare the test months.

6. **The stop-size curve (slide 55).** Sweep stops from 0.05% to 2.4% in 0.05 steps on each timeframe and plot profit after costs against stop size. With a 0.075% fee each way plus the buy-sell gap, the smallest stops should lose the most; the test is where the curve turns, and whether that point is the same on the choosing days and the unseen days.

7. **The best stop read from the worst moves is hindsight (slide 62).** Pick a stop from each strategy's older trades' worst-move quantiles, judge it on unseen days, and compare with the stop that looks best when picked on the unseen days themselves. The gap is the hindsight bonus the slide warns about.

8. **Returns pile up at the stop and the target, with a long losing tail (slide 61).** Draw the histogram of per-trade returns for fixed stop/target strategies in the store and check the two piles and the shape.

9. **More free settings, more overfitting (slide 39).** Group strategies by how many settings were searched to choose them, and measure how much profit a trade drops from the choosing days to the unseen days in each group.

10. **A losing backtest means bigger live losses; a winning one only gives a chance (slide 73).** Compare the practice rooms' closed trades with the backtest rows of the same strategies over the same days: whether live results are worse than the backtest, and by how much.
