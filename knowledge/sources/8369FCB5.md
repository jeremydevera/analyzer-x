## What it is

A reading list from Jan 10, 2010, written by the Quantivity blog for retail traders who want to learn systematic, quantitative or "algorithmic" trading. The post names eleven books in three groups (behavioral finance, trading systems and money management, and one technical analysis reference), each with a one-line verdict, and sends readers on to Part 2 (mathematical finance) and Part 3 (financial modeling). It teaches no method itself: no formula, no backtest, no number about returns or costs. The comments are worth more than the post: other traders add about thirty more books, argue over whether technical indicators or liquidity matter, warn about curve fitting and genetic algorithms, and say a system should be back tested, forward tested and tested against random. The author admits the post was changed after the comments, so some comments discuss books that are no longer in it.

## Everything it teaches

Lessons from the post itself

> "As with any discipline, best approach is to get mentored by an expert. Short of that, read all the seminal works; ideally several times each."
Where: post, first paragraph.

2. The list is for retail traders, gives basic words and topics, and leans towards stocks, exchange-listed options and futures, and currencies.
> "a reading list intended for retail traders introducing standard terminology and introductory topics, with bias to equity, exchange-traded derivatives, and FX"
Where: post, first paragraph.

> "certainly none capture anywhere near the current state-of-the-art; that said, some books are better than none"

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. Technical analysis is fooled by hindsight: "much is subject to lookback bias" (post). Test: take the classic chart families the post calls seminal (moving averages, momentum and acceleration, overbought/oversold) from the Backtest v2 store, choose a setting on each coin's older days, and compare its profit on the newest unseen days with its profit on the days it was chosen on. If the unseen profit is much smaller than the chosen-on profit, the bias is real here too. Report it before and after fees.
2. Liquidity matters more than chart indicators: "you’ll hear a lot about liquidity indicators and very little about technical indicators" (awwthor), against "only a technical indicator would do that" (John). Test: on the same coins and timeframes, compare (a) a chart signal alone, (b) the same signal only when the coin's recorded order book is tight (the cost store's per-minute buy-sell gap), and (c) random entries filtered only by that same tightness. Judge on unseen days after every fee.
3. "technical traders where trading mean reversion way before the quants" (quant.this) is history, but the useful part, that snap-back rules work, can be tested: run a mean-reversion family against random entries on unseen days, per coin, before and after fees.
4. "back test, forward test and random test it" (quant.this). Test: for any strategy we pick, require all three: the older days (back test), the newest unseen days (forward test), and the same number of random entries on the same coin and days (random test). Count how many picks pass all three versus how many random picks would pass by luck.
5. The usual way to improve a system is to "incrementally try and plug in more fancy statistics and optimization", and the method is "black magic" (quantivity). Test: add filters to one base signal one at a time, and after each one measure the older-day profit and the unseen-day profit. If older-day profit keeps rising while unseen-day profit stays flat or falls, the extra tuning is fitting the past.
6. "I would not recommend tackling optimizations problems with GAs" (gappy). Test: tune the same signal's settings by a genetic search and by the plain grid, both on older days only, and compare their unseen-day results; also count how far each search's best older-day figure overstates its unseen figure.
7. Big funds run day-trading strategies "which are flat overnight" (quantivity, a rumor). Test on crypto perpetuals: take the same signals and force every trade closed before a set hour (for example before the next funding settlement) versus letting them run; compare profit after fees and funding on unseen days.
8. Price-impact models do not fit retail size: "I don’t think Almgren’s work is relevant to the retail investor" (gappy). Test: from the recorded order books, compare the $100 walk (what $100 really pays past the best price) with the plain top-of-book gap, per coin. If the walk is close to the gap on liquid coins but much wider on thin ones, the claim holds only for liquid coins.
9. "Kelly betting" as a sizing topic (post). Test: size each trade by the Kelly fraction worked out from the older days' win rate and win/loss sizes, versus flat size, and compare the unseen-day money and worst losing run; this shows whether a Kelly size chosen on the past survives the future.
10. In currencies theory is "overwhelmed by carry trade inbalances" (quantivity). The crypto version is funding: test whether holding the side that receives funding, with no other signal, makes money after fees on unseen days, against random sides.
11. Market moods matter: "regime changes and how that affects different markets and instruments" (quant.this). Test: split the unseen days by BTC's mood (up, down, flat) and see whether the same strategy's profit changes sign between moods.
