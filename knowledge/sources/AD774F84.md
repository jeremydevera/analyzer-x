## What it is

This is the front page of Rob Carver's blog about systematic (rule-based) trading, saved with the three newest posts shown in full. The posts are "AI for systematic traders - this is the least stupid way to do it." (Nov 16, 2026 5:49pm, the page's stamp, marked UTC), "Why optimising for the left tail of outcomes is like playing chess..." (Nov 04, 2026 2:10pm) and "Optimisation with costs (oh please seriously Rob, not again...) and a sort of summary" (Nov 04, 2026 1:32pm), each with "No comments". The first says where AI may and may not be used when building trading strategies; the second says how to choose between strategies or leverage levels by looking at the bad outcomes rather than the middle one; the third sets out how trading costs should go into the weights given to trading rules and instruments, and ends with his full "gold standard" weighting recipe. The sidebar holds the archive (a fourth November 2026 post, "Writing a non fiction book", is listed there but is not on the page), ten "Popular Posts" previews and a tag list.

21. The safe way to test AI ideas is the same safe way to test human ideas.
    > "In fact this is exactly the same framework that should be used in an ideal environment for testing human generated ideas."

22. There are three kinds of fitting to the past to avoid (explicit, implicit, tacit), and with AI you cannot even see whether it is happening.
    > "We still have the same three versions of overfitting we're trying to avoid: explicit, implicit and tacit. It's just that the overfitting is wrapped in an opaque blob so we don't even know if it's happening or not."

23. Trustworthy data comes straight from its source, not through the AI.
    > "To be trustworthy the data must have come direct from the source not via the AI."

    AI post

25. Check that data does not contain the future, such as economic figures revised after release or company results dated before they came out.
    > "no forward looking data (a problem for eg macro data published with a lag and then revised, and company earnings that certainly don't come on the final day of the quarter they relate to)"
    AI post

26. What the idea stage should hand over is a small piece of code that turns data into a forecast, with settings left open to be tuned (his example has a fast and a slow span).
    > "A piece of parameter-isable code implementing the strategy idea (i.e. producing a forecast) given the data as an input"
    AI post

27. That small code can be checked for errors, but it may still carry hidden knowledge of what worked before.
    > "Note that the parameter-isable code is small and therefore it's correctness can be verified. However it may well incorporate tacit fitting."
    AI post

28. After that hand-over AI is not used again; the testing system must not be written by AI or run by agents (small parts, suggestions and tests excepted).
    > "That is the last time we will touch AI in this process."
    AI post

29. The AI gets to touch exactly one thing, the forecast rule; everything around it is off limits.
    > "the model only gets to play with one thing; the parameter-isable python code that ends up getting tested as a forecasting rule"
    AI post

30. The range of settings to try is set with a little early data, made-up data or theory, and if real data is used only correlation and turnover are looked at, never profit.
    > "If real data is used, performance isn't considered, only correlation and turnover."
    AI post

31. Use an add-only list: a rule, once tested, can never be removed, so backtest results cannot be used to tweak or drop rules.
    > "an add only system where new forecasting rules once tested can't be removed from the backtest. This avoids implicit fitting in the form of 'research loops' where the results of backtests are used to refine forecasting rules, or remove them entirely."
    AI post

32. Because no rule is ever dropped, there is no need for a statistical pass mark, and no risk of throwing out a rule that works but earns too little to clear one.
    > "we're not at risk of dropping rules that may work but whose natural profitability is too low to pass an arbitrary statistical hurdle"
    AI post

33. The weights for rules and settings are fitted only on past data, by a method that knows about statistical certainty and trading costs.
    > "Backward looking explicit fitting of the combination of optimal parameters and forecasting rules. The fitting is robust - it knows about statistical significance and trading costs"
    AI post

34. Test both a strategy and its exact opposite, because people and AI already know what worked in the past (such as medium-speed trend following).
    > "Both positive and negative versions of a strategy to avoid tacit in sample fitting. This avoids the problem that both smart humans and AI already know what's worked, eg medium speed trend following."
    AI post

    > "NOTE: Smiley face was inserted by AI not me."
    AI post (appendix)

36. Picking one of several strategies, or a leverage level, means picking one of several spreads of possible outcomes (for example from re-sampling past returns).
    > "Both are equivalent to choosing which of N distributions of possible outcomes we prefer."
    Tail post

37. Picking the best middle outcome (the 50% point) is the strict expectation way, and he says it equals the Kelly-optimal choice.
    > "The strict expectation method is to pick the option with the distribution that has the best median value, eg 50% distribtional point. As I've discussed before, that is equivalent to the Kelly optimal."
    Tail post

38. Picking the best lucky outcome (the best 1% right tail) is the gambler's way and leads to high leverage, crypto-like risk or strategies that sell insurance.
    > "We might go for the option that has the best 1% right tail. That would be a high leverage option, something with high natural risk like crypto, or a short gamma strategy"
    Tail post

39. Nobody should choose like a gambler.
    > "Nobody should be a gambler"
    Tail post

40. His preferred way is the careful one: pick the option whose bad outcomes (the 1%, 10% or 25% point) are best.
    > "We might go for the option that has the best 1%, 10% or 25% tail; eg the option that will have the best outcome if bad things happen."
    Tail post

41. Picking by the very worst outcome does not work, because with enough re-samples every option's worst ends near zero, so the furthest you can go is the least-bad 1% point.
    > "so in practice the most realistic extreme version would be something like picking the option with the most optimal 1% left tail point"
    Tail post

42. The most extreme outcomes are too uncertain to trust, and a re-sample of past returns probably cannot show them.
    > "Frankly more extreme outcomes are highly uncertain, and will probably go beyond what a historical bootstrapping exercise could predict"
    Tail post

43. Choosing the least-bad worst case is how chess programs and game theory choose, treating the market as an opponent.
    > "If you view the market as your adversary, you want to have the least bad outcome in the worst possible state."
    Tail post

44. Good traders take risks they can measure but keep most of their money safe, so he would pick around the 10% or 25% point, not the 1% point.
    > "So somewhere around the 10% or 25% point seems appropriate to me."
    Tail post

45. His system first blends rules of different speeds for each instrument and gives each instrument a weight, which gives an ideal portfolio before whole-contract sizes are considered.
    > "This gives me the ideal portfolio I would trade without worrying about pesky integer contracts."
    Costs post

46. A second step, his "dynamic optimisation", deals with whole contracts and with costs.
    > "Next, I run a dynamic optimisation ('DO') which does worry about integer contracts. But it also worries about costs."
    Costs post

47. That step drops instruments that are too costly, not liquid enough, need too much leverage or cannot be traded legally.
    > "It removes instruments which I've decided are too expensive to trade (as well as those which aren't sufficiently liquid, require too much leverage, or which I can't legally trade)."
    Costs post

48. It also adds a buffer that slows how often the system trades, and a penalty on trading costs.
    > "It applies a buffer to the overall trading, which slows down the system's turnover. And it applies a trading cost penalty."
    Costs post

49. He measures cost per trade in Sharpe-ratio units and stops trading an instrument above 0.01; his three examples sit below, just under and well above that line.
    > "S&P 500 (with 0.00025 SR points of costs per trade, amongst the cheapest I have), Cheese (0.008 SR points, just inside the 0.01 threshold where I'd stop trading it) and Sugar #16 (0.09 SR points, well above my limit)"
    Costs post

50. A "speed limit" can rule out rules whose trading rate times cost per trade is too high; where every rule fails it, he gives the instrument only its slowest rules.
    > "Should I have some heuristic pre-filtering where I won't use rules where turnover*SR cost>speed limit?"
    Costs post

51. A rule's profit before costs is hard to estimate for one instrument, so he asks whether to pool results across instruments.
    > "What is the likely pre-cost SR of these rules, and does it make sense to do some kind of pooling to estimate them?"
    Costs post

52. Fast and slow versions of a rule spread risk better than the medium one, which moves closely with both.
    > "we know that fast/slow are more diversifying than medium which will be 0.8 correlation with both"
    Costs post

53. Costs should probably mean less weight in the fast rules of a costly instrument than of a cheap one.
    > "Should we apply them so we end up putting less into faster variations for Cheese than for S&P 500?"
    Costs post

54. He asks whether a fast rule on a cheap instrument is worth holding just for spread, even if it does not make money, because otherwise fast trend would not be traded at all.
    > "Should we get more diversification by, for example, putting more in fast SP500 trend, even if that doesn't actually make money"
    Costs post

55. For instrument weights, he asks whether profit before costs is roughly equal across instruments, so the weights can be set equal.
    > "What is the likely pre-cost SR of instrument performance, and is it effectively flat so it can be set to be equal across instruments?"
    Costs post

56. Way 1 of putting costs into weighting: a simple cost ceiling, usable before and alongside any other way.
    > "Heuristic: apply a maximum cost threshold. This can be applied before, and in addition to, any of the methods below."
    Costs post

57. Way 2: ignore costs and use profit before costs (which he calls silly).
    > "Ignore costs, use gross returns."
    Costs post

58. Way 3: use profit after costs; when pooling across instruments, pool the before-cost results and then subtract each instrument's own cost.
    > "if you are pooling across instruments, that implies you should probably pool gross returns then subtract instrument specific costs"
    Costs post

59. Way 4: use only costs, treating every option's profit before costs as equal (also called silly).
    > "Just use costs - set gross returns to some arbitrary equal number."
    Costs post

60. Way 5: subtract costs times a factor above 1, because costs are more certain than profits, though the right factor is hard to find.
    > "Subtract costs from gross returns, and multiply costs by a factor >1. This reflects the fact costs are more certain than gross returns."
    Costs post

61. Way 6: work out weights on before-cost profit, then adjust them for cost afterwards (handy when pooling weights rather than returns).
    > "Calculate weights using gross returns, and adjust subsequently for cost levels"
    Costs post

62. Way 7, his new favourite: shrink the estimated before-cost Sharpe ratio toward the average, then subtract costs, which respects that costs are more certain without picking a factor.
    > "Shrink the SR estimates. Then subtract costs from the shrunk SR. This reflects the fact costs are more certain than gross returns but without the need to work out a factor as in option 5."
    Costs post

63. He judges ways 2 and 4 silly and ways 5 and 6 too complicated.
    > "Some of these seem silly (2, 4). Some of them seem over complicated (5, 6)."
    Costs post

64. Today he largely ignores costs in his weights except through the speed limit, and simply does not trade anything above 0.01 whatever its weight.
    > "And things with SR cost over 0.01 will not actually be traded in DO, irrespective of their instrument weights."
    Costs post

65. A hard cut at one cost level is ugly; costlier instruments should get gradually less weight, but not one for one.
    > "Probably, as instruments get more expensive, there should be an impact on forecast weights but not neccessarily in a complete 1:1 way."
    Costs post

66. His earlier research found that the trading step does not fully slow down costly instruments.
    > "But my previous research found that DO isn't completely effective at reducing the trading speed of expensive instruments."
    Costs post

67. The trading step does not try to predict each instrument; it looks for the cheapest way to hold a portfolio that reflects his before-cost views.
    > "with DO we are (kind of) not trying to predict the returns of any individual instrument, but find the most cost effective way to implement a portfolio which represents our opinion on pre-cost price movements"
    Costs post

68. His new recipe for rule weights: cap the cost used at 0.01, pool before-cost results, use each instrument's own cost, shrink, subtract cost, optimise, and leave anything above 0.01 out of trading.
    > "Set a ceiling on SR costs used for the optimisation at 0.01 SR"
    Costs post

69. The recipe softens the hard cut because he updates which instruments are traded (by current cost) much more often than the weights, so a costly instrument is at worst weighted as if it cost 0.01.
    > "I change my instrument overrides based on current costs much more frequently than my forecast weights."
    Costs post

70. Instrument weights use the same recipe without pooling.
    > "we can use a similar approach for those except there is no pooling"
    Costs post

71. The outer layer of his weighting only ever uses the past: it is redone each year on a 30-year window that first grows and then rolls.
    > "does rolling pure out of sample optimisation, rerunning each year on a 30 year expanding then rolling window"
    Costs post

72. A 40-year exponentially weighted window is about the same as a 28-year plain window, which is easier to code.
    > "in this post I recommended a 40 year EMA but that's equivalent to a 28 year SMA and this is nicer to code"
    Costs post

73. The pooling layer runs three weightings per instrument (its own results, its asset group's pooled results, all instruments pooled), all with the instrument's own costs, and averages them.
    > "We then take an average of those three weights."
    Costs post

74. The reverse-weights layer flips any rule that lost money before costs, so the method has no built-in belief about which way a rule should work.
    > "This means we have no prior on whether a forecast has (pre-cost) positive p&l, thus reducing tacit fitting and opening up the possibility of finding strategies we didn't realise would work."
    Costs post

75. Grouping similar rules must happen inside the flipping step, not outside it.
    > "## Clustering must go INSIDE reverse weights"
    Costs post

76. The grouping step aims for groups of six.
    > "That's a clustering, with a target cluster size of six"
    Costs post

77. Inside each group, shrink the Sharpe ratios by 0.5 and the correlations by 0.75, more with under five years of data, then subtract costs.
    > "use shrinkage for optimisation with SR shrinkage 0.5, correlation 0.75 unless you have less than five years of data in which case use higher shrinkage"
    Costs post

78. Instrument weights use exactly the same code minus pooling.
    > "The instrument weights code will be exactly the same, but without the pooling step."
    Costs post

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** This is the main subject of the Costs post. He measures cost per trade in Sharpe-ratio units and stops trading anything above 0.01. His trading step drops instruments that are too costly, slows trading with a buffer and charges a cost penalty. He lists seven ways to put costs into weighting and prefers shrinking the before-cost estimate and then subtracting each instrument's own cost, because "costs are more certain than gross returns". He warns that a hard cut at one cost level is ugly and that his trading step does not fully slow costly instruments. The AI post adds that the fitting step must know about trading costs.

**Overfitting.** The AI post names three kinds to avoid, "explicit, implicit and tacit", and says AI hides them in "an opaque blob". His defences: data straight from the source with no hidden fitting; AI only writes the small forecast rule; settings ranges chosen without looking at profit; an add-only list so no rule is ever dropped after a backtest; fitting only on the past; and testing a strategy together with its opposite. The Costs post's reverse-weights step serves the same end ("thus reducing tacit fitting").

**Testing on unseen data.** The Costs post's outer layer "does rolling pure out of sample optimisation", re-run each year on a 30-year window that first grows and then rolls. The AI post requires "Backward looking explicit fitting". Neither post gives a separate held-back test period or a pass/fail test; the AI post says that because no rule is dropped, a statistical "hurdle" is not needed.

**Position size.** Only touched on. The Tail post says the median choice "is equivalent to the Kelly optimal". The Costs post says the trading step works in whole ("integer") contracts after the ideal portfolio is set. There is no rule on how much to put into one trade.

**Leverage.** The Tail post treats choosing a leverage level as choosing between re-sampled outcome spreads, and says the gambler's best-right-tail choice "would be a high leverage option"; he would choose by the 10% or 25% bad-outcome point. The Costs post's trading step drops instruments that "require too much leverage". No leverage number is given.

**Stops.** Not covered. (The sidebar tag list shows "stop loss (2)", meaning two older posts carry that tag, but none is on this page.)

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. **Choose by the 10% or 25% bad-outcome point, not the middle (Tail post).** For each coin x timeframe in the Backtest v2 or Backtest v4 store, re-sample each candidate strategy's OLDER trades many times, pick one strategy by the median ending wallet, one by the 25% point and one by the 10% point, and judge all three on the trades after the split (v4's unseen 30%, v2's newest 15 days). If his claim holds, the 10%/25% picks lose less or show smaller worst losing runs on the unseen days. The same test works for leverage: replay one strategy's trades at several leverage levels, with each level's liquidation distance, and compare which level each choosing rule prefers.
2. **Costs are more certain than profits before costs (Costs post).** For every strategy, compare its per-trade cost (`rt_paid`, or the per-minute cost store) on the older and unseen halves against its per-trade profit before costs on the same halves. If he is right, the cost figures carry over between halves far better than the before-cost profit does.
3. **Shrink the before-cost estimate, then subtract cost (way 7).** Rank strategies on their older days three ways (net profit, before-cost profit only, before-cost profit shrunk by half and then cost subtracted) and see which ranking's top picks do best on unseen days. The text gives 0.5 for Sharpe shrinkage; we would have to decide what "shrink" means for our per-trade figures, and say so.
4. **A cost ceiling helps (way 1, the 0.01 line).** Express each coin's round-trip cost against its own per-trade spread of returns (our choice of measure, since the page does not define an "SR point") and check whether strategies above a ceiling lose more on unseen days than those below. Then check his warning against a hard cut: do results worsen smoothly with cost, or fall off a cliff?
5. **The speed limit: trading rate x cost (Costs post).** For each strategy, multiply trades per day by cost per trade and check whether that product explains unseen-day losses better than cost alone or trading rate alone.
6. **Test the opposite of every strategy (AI post, reverse weights).** For each v2 or v4 strategy, flip its trades (long becomes short, same entry and exit times) and compare before-cost results. If a strategy and its opposite are close to mirror images before costs, the "edge" is only what costs take; if a losing strategy's opposite earns more than its costs, it is worth keeping flipped.
7. **Add-only beats pick-and-drop (AI post).** Compare a portfolio that keeps every rule ever tested, weighted only on past data, against the same rules where losers were dropped after looking at results, both judged on later days. The picker lab's walk-forward days are a ready place to run this.
8. **Fast and slow trend versions spread risk better than medium (0.8 correlation).** On our candles, build the same trend rule at a fast, medium and slow setting on one coin and measure the correlation of their daily results; check whether medium really sits about 0.8 with both on crypto.
9. **Pool before-cost results across coins, then use each coin's own cost (Costs post).** Choose weights from one coin's own history, from its group's pooled history and from all coins pooled, average the three as he does, and compare on unseen days against using the coin's own history alone.
10. **Ordinary machine-learning guesses do badly because markets change (AI post).** Check the stored machine-learning strategy rows on the days after they were chosen against the hand-made rules on the same days.
