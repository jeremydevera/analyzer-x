## What it is

quantstrat is a free package for the R language that lets you write a trading strategy and test it on past prices. You build a strategy in three steps that are kept apart: indicators (numbers worked out from prices, like a moving average), signals (moments when those numbers say "maybe act now") and rules (what order to send, and how big). It keeps a record of orders as well as filled trades, so you can study orders that never filled. The README walks through one example, a 50-day and 200-day moving average cross on AAPL from Dec 31, 1999, and prints its 17 buys and sells. It ends with a warning, quoting Lopez de Prado, that a backtest cannot be unseen and must not be used as a research tool.

44. A signal is only a moment when you might want to act; you may not be able to act on it.

45. Signals, like indicators, can be worked out in bulk or live, and must not depend on positions or trades.

46. Rules are what turn prices, indicators, signals and the state of your account into orders.

47. Trade size, how fills are simulated and how orders are made and managed are kept apart from the indicators and signals.

48. Rules, unlike indicators and signals, normally depend on what happened before, and see all earlier prices and the current positions when they run.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** Not covered. The README never mentions fees, commissions, slippage, the gap between buy and sell prices, or funding. Its 17 printed fills show a price and a size and nothing else, so the example's result is before any cost. The nearest it comes is naming "fill simulation" as something rules handle, saying an order "may or may not become transactions", and saying how often you change waiting limit orders affects results.

**Overfitting.** Not covered by name; the word does not appear. The nearest point is the warning: "A backtest cannot be unseen" and "Do not research under the influence of a backtest", plus a pointer to a paper on starting from a hypothesis. It also says settings "are likely to affect strategy performance" and that they can be passed in at run time, but it presents this as a feature and gives no warning about trying many settings.

**Testing on unseen data.** Not covered. The README describes no split into older and newer days and no held-out test; its one example is tested on one stretch of history. "Cannot be unseen" is about the researcher having seen a result, not about held-out data. The fetch-tool file list (not the authors' words) names demo scripts with "walk.forward" in the name, but none was opened, so nothing is known about what they do.

**Position size.** Barely covered. The example uses a fixed 100 shares in and "all" out, on an account of 1,000,000, with no link between the two. The README says sizing belongs in the rules, kept apart from indicators and signals, and that rules can see the account and current positions. It gives no sizing method.

**Leverage.** Not covered. The only related field is `multiplier=1` in the stock() call, which the README does not explain.

**Stops.** Not covered as a stop-loss. The example exits only on the opposite crossover. "Stops and Reverse" in the README means flipping from long to short on that crossover (its commented-out rules), not a protective stop. "Risk management" is named as one thing a rule can do, with no detail. The fetch-tool file list (not the authors' words) names demo scripts about stop-losses, trailing stops and take-profits, but they were not opened.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. The 50/200 moving average cross itself: buy at market when the 50 is at or above the 200, sell everything when it falls below. The README shows it only before costs on one stock; we could run the same rule on our coins' daily candles with every fee, the minute's own order book and funding, and see whether anything is left after costs.
2. "indicator parameters such as moving average periods or thresholds are likely to affect strategy performance." We could measure how much one family's profit moves across its settings on the same coin, and whether the best setting on older days stays best on newer days.
3. "the performance of strategies are often affected by how often resting limit orders are changed / replaced / canceled." With Backtest v3's waiting orders, we could compare leaving a waiting order alone against moving it every candle.
4. "These signals are points in time at which you may want to take some action, even though you may not be able to." We could count, per strategy, the share of signals that never became trades (refused by the cost check, the price having run away, a closed market, an order that never filled), and how that share changes the result.
5. Order-to-fill ratio: we could count orders placed against orders filled for v3's waiting orders, per coin and timeframe.
6. Indicators and signals must not depend on positions or trades. We could check that each of our signals gives the same value on a candle whether worked out on the whole history or on history cut at that candle.
