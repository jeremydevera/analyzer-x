## What it is

A short book by Marcos López de Prado (ADIA Lab) arguing that almost all research on "factor investing" (buying stocks
with a trait such as cheapness or recent strength because the trait is said to earn extra return) only shows that two
things moved together, never that one causes the other. He splits false findings into two kinds: type-A, where noise is
mistaken for a pattern (mostly from trying many versions and keeping the best, "p-hacking" and backtest overfitting),
and type-B, where a real pattern is mistaken for a cause (mostly from leaving out, or wrongly adding, a variable in the
model). He teaches the basic tools of cause-and-effect research (interventions, random trials, natural experiments,
causal graphs, the backdoor and front-door rules), runs three small computer experiments showing how a standard
regression reports a false effect, and concludes that factor investing has failed so far and should be rebuilt on
stated, testable mechanisms. It says a backtest, however good, is weak evidence and cannot prove why a strategy works.

## Everything it teaches

**Back cover and Abstract**

1. Almost every published factor-investing paper only claims that things move together, while its model quietly claims a cause.
   > "Virtually all journal articles in the factor investing literature make associational claims, in denial of the causal content of factor models."

   Where: back cover blurb and Abstract.
2. The papers draw no map of what causes what, defend their model with correlations, and propose no test that could prove their reason wrong.
   > "Authors do not identify the causal graph consistent with the observed phenomenon, they justify their chosen model specification in terms of correlations, and they do not propose experiments for falsifying causal mechanisms."

   Where: back cover blurb and Abstract.
3. Without a theory of cause the findings are probably false, because of heavy backtest overfitting and wrong model choices.
   > "Absent a causal theory, their findings are likely false, due to rampant backtest overfitting and incorrect specification choices."

   Where: back cover blurb and Abstract.
4. There are two kinds of false claim, type-A and type-B, and both keep the field stuck at only describing what it sees.
   > "This Element differentiates between type-A and type-B spurious claims, and explains how both types prevent factor investing from advancing beyond its current phenomenological stage."

   Where: back cover blurb and Abstract.

**Chapter 2, Association vs Causation**

16. The saying that moving together is not cause is true, but it does not say why things move together.
    > "does not explain why association exists, and its relation to causation."

    Where: Section 2.
17. Two things are linked ("associated") when knowing one tells you something about the other.
    > "When two variables are associated, observing the value of one conveys information about the value of the other"

    Where: Section 2.
18. Ice cream sales and drownings move together; to ask whether one causes the other you need the idea of stepping in and changing one.
    > "Answering that question requires the introduction of a more nuanced concept than conditional probability: an intervention."

    Where: Section 2.
19. The real machinery that makes the data is a set of equations; the equation by which X makes Y is the "causal mechanism".
    > "The structural equation by which X causes Y is called a causal mechanism."

    Where: Section 2.
20. That machinery is almost never known, so researchers have to work backwards from probabilities.
    > "Unfortunately, the data-generating process responsible for observations is rarely known."

    Where: Section 2.
21. Ordinary econometrics has no words for forcing a value, which is why Judea Pearl invented the do-operator, do[X = x].
    > "Econometrics lacks the language to represent interventions, that is, setting the value of X (Chen and Pearl 2013)."

    Where: Section 2.
22. X causes Y when forcing X to a value makes Y's value more likely than it would be anyway.
    > "Probabilistically, a variable X is said to cause a variable Y when setting the value of X to x increases the likelihood thatY will take the valuey."

    Where: Section 2.
23. Forcing ice cream sales up does not raise drownings, so ice cream is not a cause; forcing smoking up does raise lung cancer, so smoking is.
    > "In contrast, smoking tobacco is a cause of lung cancer"

    Where: Section 2.
24. A causal graph is a drawing with arrows and no loops that says which things affect which, and in which direction.
    > "The causal structure of a system can be represented through a directed acyclic graph, also denoted a causal graph."

    Where: Section 2.
25. Stepping in means cutting every arrow that points into X; that is what economists mean by all else equal (ceteris paribus).
    > "This is the meaning of the ceteris paribus assumption, which is of critical importance to economists."

    Where: Section 2.
26. A confounder (here, the weather) causes both X and Y and makes them move together with no arrow between them; that link is called "noncausal".
    > "For this reason, this type of association is denoted noncausal."

    Where: Section 2 (Figure 1).
27. In the weather example, banning ice cream would change nothing for drownings.
    > "banning the sale of ice cream"

    Where: Section 2.
28. Conclusion one: cause is a matter of mechanisms and stepping in, beyond what statistics on observed data can say.
    > "connected to mechanisms and interventions, and distinct from the concept of association"

    Where: Section 2 (the five conclusions).
29. Conclusion two: a direct cause always shows up as a link in the data, but two causes can cancel each other so a real cause shows no link.
    > "There are causal structures where one cause may cancel another, resulting in total causation without association."

    Where: Section 2, footnote 6.
30. Conclusion three: cause has a direction.
    > "Third, unlike association, causality is directional, as represented by the arrows of the causal graph."

    Where: Section 2 (the five conclusions).
31. Conclusion four: cause has an order in time; X is set first, then Y follows.
    > "Fourth, unlike association, causality is sequential."

    Where: Section 2 (the five conclusions).
32. Conclusion five: only the causal graph tells you which other things an all-else-equal claim is holding still.
    > "The causal graph shows what “other things ” are kept equal by the intervention."

    Where: Section 2 (the five conclusions).
33. Graphs with loops and graphs without loops can both be used; neither is better, it depends on the goal.
    > "Neither representation (cyclic or acyclic) is better"

    Where: Section 2, footnote 5.

**Chapter 3, The Three Steps of Scientific Discovery**

34. Discovery has three steps: notice a pattern, propose a testable cause, then let others try to prove it wrong.
    > "the theoretical step, where researchers propose a testable causal mechanism responsible for the observed pattern"

    Where: Section 3.
35. Step one only notices that things move together, without asking why; machine-learning predictions live at this step.
    > "In the phenomenological step, researchers observe associated events, without exploring the reason for that association."

    Where: Section 3.1.
36. Step one reasons by induction: it assumes, for an unknown reason, that the pattern will happen again.
    > "At this step, inference occurs by logical induction, because the problem situation rests on the conclusion that, for some unknown reason, the phenomenon will reoccur."

    Where: Section 3.1.
37. His example: the gap between buy and sell prices widens when buying and selling are unbalanced, and that widening often comes before a jump in volatility; seeing this is not yet a theory.
    > "and that the widening of bid-ask spreads often precedes a rise in intraday volatility"

    Where: Section 3.1.
38. Step two picks the least far-fetched explanation among the competitors.
    > "Observations cannot be explained by a hypothesis more extraordinary than the observations themselves, and of various hypotheses the least extraordinary must be preferred"

    Where: Section 3.2.
39. The explanation must spell out every link from X to Y so each link can be tested on data.
    > "where the validity of each causal link and causal path can be tested empirically"

    Where: Section 3.2.
40. Wolfgang Pauli's three kinds of explanation; the third can never be confirmed or denied.
    > "correct, wrong, and not even wrong"

    Where: Section 3.2.
41. Data and statistics alone cannot produce a theory; the scientist has to add assumptions from outside the data.
    > "however data and statistical tools are not enough to produce a theory"

    Where: Section 3.2.
42. The PIN theory (probability of informed trading): market makers who keep losing to better-informed traders widen their buy-sell gap to protect themselves, provide less liquidity, and volatility rises.
    > "As a protection against losses, market makers react to orderﬂow imbalance by charging a greater premium for selling the option to be adversely selected (that premium is the bid-ask spread)."

    Where: Section 3.2.
43. PIN counts as science because it names an exact chain of steps, each made of measurable things that can be tested one by one.
    > "Two features make the PIN theory scientiﬁc"

    Where: Section 3.2.
44. A "theory" in mathematics (built from axioms) is a different thing and cannot be proven wrong by data.
    > "Mathematicians use the term theory with a different meaning than scientists."

    Where: Section 3.2.
45. Step three is done by people who did not write the theory: they work out what must be true if it is right and test that.
    > "In the falsiﬁcation step, researchers not involved in the formulation of the theory independently"

    Where: Section 3.3.
46. Each experiment attacks one link of the chain.
    > "focuses on falsifying one particular link in the chain of events involved in the causal mechanism, applying the tools of mediation analysis"

    Where: Section 3.3.
47. First way to prove a theory wrong: show that stepping in on X does nothing, so the original pattern was noncausal or a fluke.
    > "the phenomenon originally observed in step (i) was a statistical ﬂuke"

    Where: Section 3.3.
48. Second way: the theory predicts a certain result under stepping in and the result is different. Third way: show that links the theory says must exist in the data are not there (machine learning can do this).
    > "and then apply machine learning techniques to show that those associations do not exist"

    Where: Section 3.3.
49. For PIN, a test could split stocks at random into two groups and send buy orders into one group only.
    > "a researcher may split a list of stocks randomly into two groups"

    Where: Section 3.3.
50. A theory that passes has only survived for now.
    > "the research community concludes that the theory has (temporarily) survived falsiﬁcation"

    Where: Section 3.3.
51. A field study can look inside market makers' own programs: those that do not widen their gap when flow is unbalanced die out.
    > "succumb to Darwinian competition"

    Where: Section 3.3.
52. Big fund managers already run random experiments on their brokers (algo-wheel experiments) but keep the results private.
    > "Sophisticated large asset managers routinely conduct so-called algo-wheel experiments to assess broker performance, however the results from these controlled experiments are rarely made public"

    Where: Section 3.3, footnote 9.
53. The strongest test is a "risky forecast" about something never seen before; that is a true out-of-sample test.
    > "Successful theories answer questions about previously observed events, as well as never-before observed events."

    Where: Section 3.3.
54. PIN predicted that liquidity could fail about fourteen years before the 2010 flash crash, and traders who used it profited from that crash.
    > "the PIN theory implied the possibility of failures in the provision of liquidity approximately fourteen years before the ﬂash crash of 2010 took place"

    Where: Section 3.3.
55. Science swaps the guess that Y will follow X because it did before for the knowledge that Y follows X through a tested mechanism.
    > "Science is essential to human understanding in that it replaces unreliable inductive reasoning"

    Where: Section 3.4.
56. Simpler theories are better because they are easier to test.
    > "Parsimonious theories are preferable, because they are easier to falsify, as they involve controlling for fewer variables"

    Where: Section 3.4.
57. The simplest surviving theory raises new questions, and the three steps start again.
    > "re-starting a new iteration of the three-step process, which will result in a better theory yet"

    Where: Section 3.4.
58. The older Socratic method also has three steps, but it argues about definitions, not about observed causes.
    > "However, both methods differ in three important aspects."

    Where: Section 3.4.
59. Hard maths, measurements or peer review do not make a claim scientific.
    > "A claim does not become scienti ﬁc by virtue of its use of complex mathematics, its reliance on measurements, or its submission to peer review."

    Where: Section 3.4.
60. Michael Faraday knew almost no maths and was a great scientist because he designed experiments that showed mechanisms.
    > "did not reach beyond the simplest of algebra"

    Where: Section 3.4, footnote 13.
61. Philosophers disagree on where science ends, but agree a scientific theory must be testable in some sense.
    > "for a theory to be scientiﬁc, it must be falsiﬁable in some wide or narrow sense"

    Where: Section 3.4.
62. A statistical test (p-value against a chosen false-alarm rate) is a proof by contradiction that is only probably right.
    > "this proposition is categorized as a stochastic proof by contradiction"

    Where: Section 3.4.
63. A test can reject an idea but never prove it; failing to reject proves nothing.
    > "whereby a hypothesis can never be accepted, but it can be rejected"

    Where: Section 3.4.
64. Ronald Fisher said the same: the null hypothesis (the idea that nothing is going on) is never proven, only possibly disproven.
    > "the null hypothesis is never proved or established, but is possibly disproved, in the course of experimentation"

    Where: Section 3.4, footnote 15.
65. Testing a claim of cause always needs an experiment (which, under some conditions, can be simulated).
    > "but either way the statistical falsiﬁcation of a causal claim always requires an experiment"

    Where: Section 3.4.
66. Most statisticians and economists are trained only to test claims that things move together, not cause.
    > "associational claims and have a limited understanding of the statistical falsiﬁcation of causal claims in general"

    Where: Section 3.4.

**Chapter 4, Causal Inference**

67. The difference you observe between two groups is not the true effect: it is the effect on those who got the treatment plus a "self-selection bias", and the first part needs a "what if" that can never be observed.
    > "The fundamental problem of causal inference is that computing A TT requires estimating the counterfactual"

    Where: Section 4 (equations 2 and 3).
68. That self-selection bias can be big enough to fool the researcher completely.
    > "to the point of misleading the researcher"

    Where: Section 4.
69. In the ice cream case the true effect is about zero and nearly all of the observed difference is that bias.
    > "hence ATT ≈ 0, and the observed difference is largely due to SSB."

    Where: Section 4.
70. Three kinds of study can remove the bias.
    > "These studies can be largely grouped into three types: interventional studies, natural experiments, and simulated interventions."

    Where: Section 4.
71. A controlled experiment (the first recorded one by Ibn al-Haytham, on light) needs direct control of every variable.
    > "the researcher must have direct control of all the variables involved in the data- generating process"

    Where: Section 4.1.
72. When not everything can be controlled, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) assigns units to groups at random so the groups are alike; repeating it on subsamples measures the uncertainty, and blinding removes more bias.
    > "Such random assignment aims to create two groups that are as comparable as possible, so that any difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment."

    Where: Section 4.1.
73. With random assignment the bias is zero and the observed difference equals the true average effect.
    > "In conclusion, under random assignment, the observed difference matches both A TT and A TE"

    Where: Section 4.1 (equations 4 to 6).
74. In a natural experiment, nature (or something outside the researcher's control) does the random assignment; the researcher must find the two groups.
    > "Put differently, Nature performs the do-operation"

    Where: Section 4.2.
75. Case-control, cohort and synthetic-control studies are not natural experiments, because nothing is assigned at random.
    > "are not proper natural experiments because there is no random assignment of units to groups"

    Where: Section 4.2.
76. Looking back over history (a retrospective cohort study) is cheap and fast but open to publication bias and survivorship bias.
    > "Retrospective cohort studies are usually cheap and fast, however they are more vulnerable to publication bias and survivorship bias, among other problems."

    Where: Section 4.2, footnote 18.
77. Regression discontinuity: compare units just above and just below a cut-off, which are alike except by noise.
    > "The critical assumption behind RDD is that groups (a) and (b) are comparable in everything but the slight difference in the assignment variable"

    Where: Section 4.2.
78. Crossover study: the treatment is removed for a while and returned, so each unit is its own control; leave long enough gaps so old treatments do not leak into new ones, and watch for the order mattering.
    > "wash-out periods should be observed between treatments, to avoid that past treatments confound the estimated effects of new treatments (carryover effects)"

    Where: Section 4.2.
79. Difference-in-differences compares before-after changes of a treated and an untreated group; it only works if both would have moved alike, which can be checked on the time before the treatment.
    > "DID relies on the “equal-trends assumption,” namely that no time-varying differences exist between treatment and control groups."

    Where: Section 4.2.
80. Each weaker kind of study needs stronger assumptions, so its conclusions are less general.
    > "Each approach demanded stronger assumptions than the previous one, with the corresponding cost in terms of generality of the conclusions."

    Where: Section 4.3.
81. When no experiment is possible, a "simulated intervention" uses an assumed causal graph to remove the bias from plain observations, valid only if the graph is right.
    > "The hypothesized causal graph encodes the information needed to remove from observations the SSB introduced by confounders, under the assumption that the causal graph is correct."

    Where: Section 4.3.
82. A simulated intervention cannot prove the graph it assumes; it can only disprove it, by finding a contradiction.
    > "In simulated interventions, the causal graph is part of the assumptions, and one cannot prove what one is assuming."

    Where: Section 4.3.
83. "Causal discovery" searches observed data for the shape of the graph; data almost never settle it but can narrow the choices.
    > "it often contains information helpful in reducing the number of possible structures of interdependence among variables"

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
84. Constraint-based methods: the PC algorithm assumes no hidden confounders; the FCI algorithm still works with them.
    > "The FCI algorithm gives asymptotically correct results even in the presence of latent confounders."

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
85. Score-based methods such as GES get the right graph with enough data, but maybe not with the data you have, and depend on the order variables are given.
    > "GES is also known to be sensitive to the initial ordering of variables."

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
86. Functional causal models tell the direction of an arrow from the fact that the noise is independent of the cause only in the true direction.
    > "because the independence condition between ε and X holds only for the true causal direction"

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
87. Causal graphs can even be built from text, such as news articles stating that X leads to Y.
    > "Causal graphs can also be derived from nonnumerical data."

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
88. Machine learning can first find which variables matter (mean-decrease accuracy, surrogate models, Shapley values); then causal discovery arranges the arrows.
    > "Machine learning is a powerful tool for causal discovery."

    Where: Section 4.3.1.
89. Do-calculus is a complete set of rules for turning a question about forcing X into ordinary probabilities, with the graph telling you what to condition on.
    > "Do-calculus is a complete axiomatic system that allows researchers to estimate do-operators by means of conditional probabilities"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.
90. Three shapes: a confounder points into both X and Y; a collider is pointed into by both; a mediator sits between them on the path.
    > "A variable Z is a collider with respect to X and Y when the causal relationships are reversed"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.1.
91. A path is blocked by an un-conditioned collider or by conditioning on a non-collider; a link in the data means cause only when every non-causal path is blocked.
    > "Association implies causation only if all noncausal paths are blocked."

    Where: Section 4.3.2.1.
92. The right set of variables to condition on can only be chosen with the causal graph in hand.
    > "The catch is, deciding which variables belong in S requires knowledge of the causal graph that comprises all the paths between X and Y"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.1.
93. Backdoor rule: condition on a set that blocks every back-door path into X and holds nothing caused by X; there can be several such sets.
    > "conditioning on S blocks all backdoor paths between X and Y"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.2.
94. Front-door rule: when the confounder is hidden, a mediator that carries all of X's effect can still give the effect.
    > "the front-door criterion allows researchers to estimate the causal effect with the help of a mediator"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.3.
95. Instrumental variable: with no mediator, find a W that moves X, reaches Y only through X, and shares no back-door with Y.
    > "as long as researchers can ﬁnd a variable W that turns X into a collider"

    Where: Section 4.3.2.4.

**Chapter 5, Causality in Econometrics**

96. A review of six econometrics textbooks found most deny that their equations are about cause and mix up cause with association.
    > "most textbooks deny the causal content of econometric equations, and confuse causation with association"

    Where: Section 5.
97. Least squares is one-directional (Y on X gives a different line from X on Y), so using it already assumes X causes Y.
    > "The vast majority of econometric models rely on least-squares estimators, hence implying causal relationships, not associational relationships"

    Where: Section 5.1.
98. A Deming (orthogonal) regression, which puts error on both sides, is the one that only describes association.
    > "a type of errors-in-variables model that attributes errors to both X and Y"

    Where: Section 5.1.
99. The causal graph must come first and decide the model, never the other way round.
    > "hence the identi ﬁcation of a causal graph should always precede any choice of model speci ﬁcation."

    Where: Section 5.1.
100. Economists are taught debunked ways of searching for a model, such as stepwise selection, which is selection bias from many tests.
     > "Instead, they have been taught debunked speci ﬁcation-searching procedures, such as the stepwise algorithm (an instance of selection bias under multiple testing"

     Where: Section 5.1.
101. A regression slope is unbiased only if the error is unrelated to X.
     > "This is known as the exogeneity condition."

     Where: Section 5.2.
102. Read the textbook way (the slope is just a line through the data), a regression cannot say what happens if you change X.
     > "A ﬁrst ﬂaw of this approach is that it cannot answer interventional questions, hence it is rarely useful for building theories."

     Where: Section 5.2.
103. Read the causal way, the slope is the effect of forcing X, but only if the model is correctly set up.
     > "the validity of this assumption is contingent on the model being correctly speciﬁed"

     Where: Section 5.2.
104. Economists estimate the slope as an association and then use it as a cause, which produces false claims.
     > "while at the same time they interpret and use the estimated β as if it were a causal concept, leading to spurious claims"

     Where: Section 5.2.
105. "Granger causality" (past X helps predict Y) is a bad name: it measures order in time, not cause.
     > "The term Granger causality is an unfortunate misnomer."

     Where: Section 5.3.
106. Coming first is needed for cause but is not enough.
     > "However, sequentiality is a necessary, non-sufﬁcient condition for causality"

     Where: Section 5.3.
107. By Granger's test a rooster's crow causes dawn, yet silencing the rooster shows it does not.
     > "And yet, it is trivial to falsify the claim that a rooster’s crow is a cause of dawn"

     Where: Section 5.3.
108. The test also fails with a shared cause, suffers selection bias from trying many lag choices, and assumes a straight-line link.
     > "A third problem is that the test itself is susceptible to selection bias"

     Where: Section 5.3.
109. Granger's 1969 paper is cited over 33,000 times and its confusion fills factor research.
     > "with over 33,000 citations"

     Where: Section 5.3.
110. A few economists brought real cause-and-effect methods (instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, the 2021 Nobel for natural experiments), but asset pricing ignores them.
     > "On the other hand, asset pricing remains to this day staunchly oblivious to rigorous causal reasoning."

     Where: Section 5.3.

**Chapter 6, Causality in Factor Investing**

111. Factor investing means betting on measurable traits that supposedly explain why some securities do better than others.
     > "Factor investing can be de ﬁned as the investment approach that targets the exposure to measurable characteristics"

     Where: Section 6.
112. The two standard methods (Fama-MacBeth regressions and Fama-French sorted long-short portfolios) try to explain differences between securities, not changes over time.
     > "but rather to explain differences in average returns across securities"

     Where: Section 6.
113. Even the word "factor" means a cause ("doer"), yet the literature never says what causes the returns.
     > "Semantically, a factor is a cause responsible, in total or in part, for an effect."

     Where: Section 6, footnote 26.
114. "Theory" in asset pricing is the mathematical kind: modern portfolio theory is proven from axioms, not tested against the world.
     > "Modern portfolio theory results are true in a mathematical sense, by virtue of proven theorems, however they are not necessarily true in a physical sense."

     Where: Section 6, footnote 27.
115. "Causal content" is the set of cause assumptions, stated or not, built into how a factor model is set up, estimated, read and used.
     > "I denote causal content the set of causal assumptions, whether declared or undeclared, that are embedded in a factor model’s speciﬁcation, estimation, interpretation, and use."

     Where: Section 6.1.
116. Sign one that factor models claim cause: someone who only wanted to describe a link would use stronger machine-learning tools than least squares.
     > "would surely use more powerful techniques from the machine learning toolbox than a least-squares estimator"

     Where: Section 6.1.
117. Sign two: they give up prediction accuracy (no ridge, LASSO or Sharpe-ratio targets) to keep the slope unbiased.
     > "Second, the estimation of β prioritizes causal interpretation over predictive power."

     Where: Section 6.1.
118. Sign three: they report p-values on the slope, a claim of a real effect, where predictors use importance scores instead.

- **Observed difference = ATT + SSB:** the plain difference between the two groups splits into the effect on the treated and the self-selection bias (equation 3); random assignment makes the bias zero (equations 4 to 6).
  > "The above equation splits the observed difference into two components, the so- called average treatment effect on the treated (A TT) and self-selection bias (SSB)."

  Where: Section 4.
- **Hypothesis test as a rule:** if the null is true the data's chance is at least alpha; the data's chance came out below alpha; so reject the null (equation 1), alpha being the false-alarm rate you chose.
  > "α denotes the targeted false positive rate"

  Where: Section 3.4.
- **Adjusting with a set S (d-separation):** the effect equals the average, over S, of the difference in Y between treated and control within each value of S (equation 7).
  > "Two variablesX and Y are d-separated by a (possibly empty) set of variablesS if, upon conditioning on S, all paths between X and Y are blocked."

  Where: Section 4.3.2.1.
- **Path rule:** a path is blocked if it passes through a collider you did NOT condition on (nor its descendants), or through a non-collider you DID condition on.
  > "A path between X and Y is blocked if either"

  Where: Section 4.3.2.1.
- **Backdoor formula:** chance of Y given forced X = sum over s of [chance of Y given X and S = s] times [chance of S = s] (equation 8); S must block every back-door path and contain no descendant of X.
  > "Then, S is a sufﬁcient adjustment set, and the causal effect of X on Y can be estimated as"

  Where: Sections 4.3.2.2 and 4.3.2.3 (the same sentence introduces both formulas).
- **Front-door formula:** sum over the mediator's values of [chance of mediator given X] times the sum over X values of [chance of Y given mediator and that X] times [chance of that X] (equation 9); three conditions: every causal path goes through S, no back-door X to S, every back-door S to Y blocked by X.
  > "(i) all causal paths from X to Y go through S"

  Where: Section 4.3.2.3.
- **Instrumental variable estimate (straight-line case):** slope of Y on W divided by slope of X on W; the plain slope of Y on X is biased by the hidden confounder.
  > "The coefﬁcient of effect (4) can be estimated from the slope of the regression line of X on W"

  Where: Section 4.3.2.4.
- **Least squares:** the estimate equals the true slope plus a term from the error (equation 10); it is unbiased only when the error averages zero given X.
  > "For the estimate to be unbiased"

  Where: Section 5.2.
- **Causal slope:** under explicit exogeneity the slope is the change in the expected Y per unit of forced change in X (equation 11).
  > "β has a causal interpretation, as the expected value of Y given an intervention that sets the value of X"

  Where: Section 5.2.
- **Granger test:** regress Y on its own past and on past X (equation 12); "X causes Y" if any past-X slope is significant.
  > "According to Granger, X causes Y if and only if at least one of the estimated coefﬁcients"

  Where: Section 5.3.
- **Bias from a missing confounder:** with X = Zδ + v and Y = Xβ + Zγ + u (all noise standard normal), fitting Y on X alone gives on average β + γδ/(1 + δ²) (equation 16, proved in Appendix A.1); the bias exists only when both γ and δ are non-zero.
  > "is the bias due to the missing confounder"

  Where: Section 6.4.2.1 and Appendix A.1.
- **Sign flip:** with both true rewards fixed and positive, a change in δ over time changes the fitted slope, and a negative enough δ makes it negative.
  > "Even if the two risk premia remain constant, changes over time in δ will change"

  Where: Section 6.4.2.1.
- **Collider covariance:** with X and Y independent standard normals and Z = X + Y + noise, the covariance of X and Y is 0, but given Z it is minus one third (the conditional variance of each is two thirds; Appendix A.2).
  > "Then, the covariance between X and Y conditional on Z is"

  Where: Section 7.2 (equations 30 and 31) and Appendix A.2.

  > "First, draw 5,000 observations from the data-generating process characterized by the structural equation model"

  Where: Section 7.1 and Snippet 1.
- **Collider:** X = noise, Y = noise, Z = X + Y + noise; fit Y on X and Z (false positive), then Y on X (no effect).

- **Confounded mediator:** X = noise, W = noise, Z = X + W + noise, Y = Z + W + noise; fit Y on X and Z gives a strongly negative slope though every effect is positive; Y on X alone gives the right positive slope. **Rules**

- **Six grades of evidence** (Figure 12), best first: 1 random trials (deduction with partial induction, very high, e.g. algo-wheel experiments); 2 natural experiments (high); 3 simulated interventions (medium); 4 econometric studies and backtests (enumerative induction, low); 5 case studies (very low); 6 expert opinion (anecdotal).
  > "Rank Type Inference Rigor Example 1 Randomized controlled trials"

  Where: Section 6.5 (Figure 12).
- **Type-A checklist:** false positives; less likely with more data for the same number of tries; fixed by multiple-testing adjustments (Hochberg 1988, Bailey and López de Prado 2014); zero expected return before costs.
  > "Type-A spuriosity has several distinct attributes"

  Where: Section 6.4.1.
- **Type-B checklist:** false positives and negatives; possible with one try; more likely with more data; not fixed by multiple-testing adjustments; needs a causal theory.
  > "Type-B spuriosity has several distinct attributes"

  Where: Section 6.4.2.
- **What a cause claim must disclose:** (i) all cause assumptions as a graph; (ii) a testable mechanism; (iii) evidence for both.
  > "(i) making all causal assumptions explicit through a causal graph"

  Where: Section 6.3.
- **Two-stage rule:** stage 1 discover the graph; stage 2 choose controls to estimate one arrow of it; never pick controls by fit.
  > "Stage (2) should be informed by stage (1), not the other way around."

  Where: Section 6.4.2.3.
- **Never control for a collider or a mediator** of the effect you want; do control for confounders (backdoor rule).
  > "Common examples of over-controlling include controlling for variables that are mediators or colliders relative to the causal path from X to Y"

  Where: Section 6.4.2.2.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** Only in passing, but pointedly. The factor index's 0.29 Sharpe ratio and 1.13% a year leave out trading
costs, market impact, the cost of borrowing stock to sell short, and management fees, and assume a 0% safe rate; with a
real 6-month rate the Sharpe ratio is below zero (Section 6.4). A type-A false factor earns zero on average "before
transaction costs and fees" (Section 6.4.1), so after costs it loses. The book gives no method or figure for measuring
costs.

**Overfitting.** A main theme. Type-A false claims come from trying many versions and keeping the best (p-hacking,
backtest overfitting, the False Strategy Theorem); the fix is to log every try and adjust for the number of tries
(family-wise error rate, false discovery rate, Hochberg, Bailey and López de Prado). It adds a second kind that this
fix cannot touch: choosing a model by how well it fits (specification-searching) gives a wrong model whatever the
number of tries, and its own experiment shows the wrong model fitting better (adjusted R-squared 0.784 against 0.144).
It does not explain the False Strategy Theorem or the deflated Sharpe ratio in this text; it only cites them.

**Testing on unseen data.** Out-of-sample data is better than in-sample, but it only protects against type-A (noise),
not type-B (a real link that is not a cause); "out-of-sample" is a matter of when data was used, not a kind of evidence
(Section 6.5). Collecting enough new data to disprove a link can take decades: 31 years of daily data for a 0.29 Sharpe
ratio (Sections 6.4 and 8.1). Getting the same backtest on another sector, country or period is not proof either
(Section 6.4.1.2). The strongest unseen-data test is a "risky forecast" a theory makes about a situation never seen
(Section 3.3).

**Position size.** Not covered, except one line saying causal mechanisms "enable factor timing, dynamic bet sizing, and
tactical asset allocation" (Section 8.3), with no rule given.

**Leverage.** Not covered.

**Stops.** Not covered. The nearest idea is "surveillance": watch the mechanism itself and get out when it weakens,
before losses are large enough for a statistical test to show a break (Section 8.3).

## Claims we could test on our own data

The book is about stock factors held for months, not crypto strategies held for hours; each test below is my reading
of how its claims would show up in this repo's Gate data.

1. **A type-A pick earns zero before costs.** Claim: a false pattern's expected return "is zero, before
   transaction costs and fees" (Section 6.4.1). Test: for picks chosen on older days, add up their unseen trades'
   profit BEFORE fees and the order-book gap, beside random entries treated the same way. If both sit near zero and
   only the costs make them negative, the picks are type-A. The repo's Oct 20, 2026 walk-forward already points this
   way (picks -$0.23 a trade, random entries -$0.22, with roughly $0.00 before fees), so this mostly repeats a known
   result; it would confirm, not refute, the book.
2. **Type-A shrinks with more data for the same number of tries; type-B grows.** Test: keep the grid fixed (same
   number of settings) and choose on 15, 30, 90, 180 and 365 older days. Measure the gap between the chosen setting's
   older profit a trade and its unseen profit a trade. A gap that shrinks as the window grows is type-A; a pick whose
   unseen result gets steadier with more data but disappears once the confounder in test 3 is added is type-B.
3. **Out-of-sample does not catch a missing confounder.** Test: for every pick that made money on unseen days, record
   BTC's return over each trade's hold and fit the trade's profit on its side times BTC's move (the book's partial correlations method, where the researcher "adds Z as a
   regressor"). If the pick's edge vanishes once BTC's move is in the model, and random entries show
   the same pattern, the "edge" was exposure to the market's direction, not the signal.
4. **Rewards that flip sign are a changing link to a missing variable.** Claim: a change in how the factor moves with
   the left-out variable can turn its fitted reward negative (Section 6.4.2.1). Test: month by month, compute each
   kept rule set's profit and the correlation between its trade side and BTC's trend that month; check whether the
   months where profit flips sign are the months where that correlation flips. This repo's picker lab found
   trend-following rule sets giving their gains back the next month, which is the kind of flip to test.
5. **The best-fitting setup is not the best setup.** Claim: the wrong model fit better (Section 7.3). Test: per coin
   and timeframe, compare the unseen result of the setting with the best older profit against a fixed default setting
   chosen before looking. If the best-fitting one does no better unseen, choosing by fit adds nothing.
6. **Black boxes swing from steady profit to steady loss.** Claim: heavily over-controlled strategies "routinely
   transition" from profits to losses (Section 6.4.2.2). Test: among Forecast v4 rule sets, count month-to-month sign
   reversals for rule sets with many conditions (win rate + trade count + money rule + stop cap + timeframe) against
   those with one or two; more reversals for the complex ones would support it.
7. **Working on many coins is not proof.** Claim: the same backtest result on other groups or periods is not causal
   evidence (Section 6.4.1.2). Test: for any rule claimed to work across coins, apply the same many-coins
   check to random entries chosen the same way and report both pass rates. The Pro Trader research already found 3 of
   1,900 random picks passing the money checks against 3 of 2,255 real ones.
8. **Filtering on an outcome can create a fake link (collider).** Claim: conditioning on something caused by both the
   signal and the result makes unrelated things look related (Sections 6.4.2.2, 7.2). In this repo, a filter computed
   from a row's own trades (for example the 2:1-after-fees rule, judged on the costs its trades actually paid), or a
   universe of only the contracts still listed today, selects on results. Test: run the same filter on random-entry
   rows; if random rows that pass the filter also look profitable on the days used to filter, the filter is producing
   the look of an edge.
9. **The book's own mechanism example (PIN) can be checked on recorded books.** Claim: unbalanced order flow makes
   market makers widen the buy-sell gap, and wider gaps come before higher volatility (Sections 3.1 to 3.3). The app
   records every contract's order book 3 seconds into every minute, so the first step (does a widening gap come before
   larger 1-minute moves over the next N minutes, compared with random minutes?) is measurable. Only the link can be
   checked this way; the book says an intervention or natural experiment is needed to show cause.
10. **How long until a result means anything.** Claim: a 0.29 annual Sharpe ratio needs 31 years of daily data to be
    significant at 95% (Section 6.4). Test: before calling a forward-tested list or a room as working, compute its daily
    Sharpe ratio and the number of days it would need to clear 95%, beside the spread of random entries. The book
    does not print the formula (it cites Bailey and López de Prado 2012), so that part would come from elsewhere.
11. **What comes first may share a cause with what follows.** Claim: Granger-style precedence is not cause (Section 5.3). Test:
    does an altcoin signal still predict its next-candle return after BTC's same-candle and previous-candle returns are
    added to the regression? If not, the signal was reading BTC.
