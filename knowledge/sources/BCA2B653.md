## What it is

It is Marcos Lopez de Prado's list of his own lectures and talks, in two tables. The first table is "Advances in Financial
Machine Learning", ten numbered lectures for a Cornell University course plus one on the Numerai tournament, and it gives
only their titles. The second table, "Recent seminars and academic lectures", lists 50 talks from 2012 to 2026, each with
a one- to six-sentence abstract. The common thread is that most investment discoveries and most backtests are false
because people try many things and report only the winner, and that better statistics, machine learning and cause-and-
effect thinking can fix it. The page is a table of contents: the lectures themselves were not downloaded.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** Trading costs (fees per trade, the buy-sell gap, slippage, funding) are not covered. The only cost words are
about the fees investors pay hedge funds ("worth a substantial portion of the fees paid to hedge funds") and about
execution: market makers widen their range against informed traders, and the Optimal Execution Horizon picks how fast
to trade ("to determine the optimal participation rate"). No cost figures are given.

**Overfitting.** This is the page's main subject. Trying many settings and keeping the best makes the result false
("Most discoveries in empirical finance are false, as a consequence of selection bias under multiple testing."); with
enough tries even random data looks significant; a backtest can be fit to any result for a fixed length of data; so the
number of tries must be reported and the test period must grow with it (MinBTL). Tuning a trading rule on a backtest
"contributes to backtest overfitting, which in turn leads to underperformance". The cures named are the Deflated Sharpe
Ratio, controlling the FWER and FDR of a whole organisation, reporting the false-positive chance, and cause-and-effect
models. Machine learning is said to overfit only "in the wrong hands".

**Testing on unseen data.** Covered in four places. A plain hold-out is "inaccurate in the context of back-test
evaluation". There is "test set overfitting" as well as "train set overfitting", meaning a test set used again and again
gets fitted too. The microstructure study separates "explanatory (in-sample) and predictive (out-of-sample)"
importance. Mean-variance portfolios are best "in-sample" and poor "out-of-sample". The page does not say how to split
the data.

**Position size.** Only at the portfolio level, never per trade: NCO, the Critical Line Algorithm, a new diversified
portfolio method that does better on new data, and the warning that risk piled along one common direction of the market
can bring "maximum risk for that portfolio size" even under the limits. No rule for how much to stake on one trade.

**Leverage.** Not covered.

**Stops.** Not covered. "Optimal Trading Rules Without Backtesting" names an "optimal trading rule (OTR)" but the
abstract does not say what the rule is made of. "How long does it take to recover from a Drawdown?" is a title; its
abstract is about judging managers by the Sharpe ratio and gives no stop or recovery figure.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. **Picking the best of many backtests gives a false winner** (entries 38, 40, 50, 54). Our stores already hold
   millions of measured settings per night. Test: count the tries behind each "best" row, then compare how the best rows
   chosen on older days do on newer, unseen days against rows picked at random from the same coin and timeframe (Backtest
   v2's older 15 days against its newest 15; Backtest v3's 90 unseen days and its 200 random runs). If the page is right,
   the best rows should do no better than random once fees are paid.
2. **The more you try, the longer the test must be (MinBTL)** (entry 54). Test: for the same family of rules, measure
   how often an older-days winner stays a winner on unseen days when the window is 30 days (Backtest v2) against 365 days
   (Backtest v3), and with 10, 100 and 1,000 settings tried. The claim predicts that more tries on a short window means
   more winners that fail later.
3. **A hold-out used again and again gets fitted too** (entries 28, 54). Test: the rooms and the picker choose rule
   sets by how they did on recent days and then keep re-choosing. Walk it forward: pick on days 1-30, judge on 31-37,
   repeat; then check whether the rule sets that looked best on many such checks keep their edge on a stretch never used
   for any choice.
4. **The Sharpe ratio misjudges skewed returns** (entries 52, 55, 59). Our trades have fixed targets and stops, so
   their returns are lopsided (for example a small target and a bigger stop wins often and loses big). Test: rank rows by
   plain Sharpe and by a Sharpe corrected for skew and fat tails, and see which ranking better predicts the unseen days.
5. **Low false positives cost you true winners (Type I against Type II)** (entry 35). Test: raise and lower a room's
   pass bar (for example 70%, 80%, 90% wins over 30 days) and count, on the unseen days that follow, both the passed rows
   that lost and the rejected rows that would have made money.
6. **Most discoveries are false; FDR above 80%** (entry 12). Test: among rows that pass a screen on older days, estimate
   how many would pass by luck from the random-entry runs' pass rate, and divide by the number that really passed. That
   ratio is our own false discovery rate.
7. **Order flow imbalance predicts the trading range** (entry 57). We record the order book every minute. Test: work
   out the buy-against-sell imbalance from the recorded books and check whether it predicts the next candle's high-low
   range better than the range of the candles before.
8. **Mean-variance loses to equal weights on new data** (entry 46). Test: across a set of coins, fit mean-variance
   weights on an older window and compare their result on the next window against equal weights.
9. **Risk piled on one common direction causes bigger losses in risk-on/risk-off swings** (entry 58). Test: from coin
   returns, measure how much of the movement the first common direction explains on calm days and on selloff days, and
   check whether a room's worst losing runs happen when its open trades all lean the same way.

## What I did not read, and doubts

- **None of the 61 lectures or talks was downloaded.** I read only the page: titles, years and short abstracts. Every
  lesson above is what a title or an abstract says, not what the slides or papers prove.
- **The ten course lectures have titles only** (entries 1-11). Their content (data, modelling, backtesting I and II) is
  unknown from this page.
- **Several abstracts promise lists they do not give**: the "7 critical mistakes" (entries 10, 42), the "three lessons"
  from COVID-19 (27), the "three" machine-learning solutions (25), the "ten" applications (36), the practical method in
  "How the Sharpe Ratio Died" (40), and what the "optimal trading rule" is made of (51).
- **The saved text has damaged characters.** The page is in the windows-1252 code page and the saved text put a
  replacement mark where curly quotes, apostrophes and accented letters were (around "factor zoo", "Draw Your
  Assumptions Before Your Conclusions", "discovery", the name of the bridge city, the word after "1/N", "economists",
  "Market Makers", "A fund", and others). I did not quote across any of them.
- **The raw page's leftover share-button frame names the SSRN paper at abstract 2150879** (the link for "The Sharp
  Razor") as *Assessing Investment Performance under Non-Normal and Serially-Correlated Returns* (a title found only in the raw file, not in the saved text). So the paper behind
  that link may carry a different title from the one the page shows. Not checked.
- **The years are the page's own** and may be the year of the talk, not of the paper.
- **One quote keeps a typo from the source**: "the number trials involved" (no "of").
