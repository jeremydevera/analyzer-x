## What it is

The publications page of Marcos Lopez de Prado. It is a table of his journal papers, books and chapters, and working papers, from 2003 to 2026, each with the authors, the journal and a two-to-four sentence summary. About a third of the papers are about how backtests fool you: trying many strategies and keeping the best one, the Sharpe ratio (profit divided by how much it swings) being inflated, and how to correct for that. Others cover machine learning in finance, building portfolios, trading costs and market impact, market makers and "flow toxicity", factor investing and cause and effect, and (lately) trustworthy AI. The page says most of his work is kept private: "The majority of our findings are kept proprietary."

## Everything it teaches

Each lesson below is read from the paper's summary on the page, not from the paper itself.

1. A Sharpe ratio found by searching has to be shrunk for the search, and there are several ways to do it; this paper puts them in one framework.
   > "We present a unified framework for alternative implementations of the Deflated Sharpe ratio, including location-only and location-scale adjustments."
   The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: A Unified Framework for Search-Adjusted Performance Inference (2026)

2. Using the plain Sharpe ratio as it comes misleads you in four ways, and the paper offers four corrected measures instead.
   > "The paper shows that naive Sharpe ratio use is flawed due to bias, low power, p-value misuse, and multiple testing."
   > "It proposes corrected measures (PSR, MinTRL, DSR, Bayesian FDR) to make inference statistically valid."
   How to Use the Sharpe Ratio / Sharpe Ratio Inference: A New Standard for Decision-Making and Reporting (2026)

3. How sure you can be of a Sharpe ratio changes when calm and wild periods come in clumps; they work out an exact answer for that case.
   > "We derive a closed-form solution to the asymptotic variance of the Sharpe ratio under GARCH returns."
   Signal-to-Noise Ratio Inference under Volatility Clustering and Heavy Tails (working paper, 2026)

4. They also work out how spread out a measured Sharpe ratio is when returns are not bell-shaped and each day leans a little on the day before.
   > "We derive an explicit asymptotic normal approximation for its plug-in estimator under weak dependence"
   On the Asymptotic Distribution of the Sharpe Ratio under Weak Dependence and Non-Gaussian Returns (working paper, 2026)

5. When results come out of a search-and-pick process, the numbers you report from the tested days cannot tell you how many of your 'discoveries' are false, and older methods that claimed the false share is low are wrong.
   > "We prove that the false discovery rate (FDR) cannot be identified from reported in-sample statistics when results arise from a search-and-selection process."
   > "can severely understate the true rate of false discoveries in finance"
   The False Discovery Rate in Finance: Identification Failure and Search-Adjusted Estimation (working paper, 2026)

6. New tools help markets only if backtests do not leak future data and false discoveries are controlled.
   > "only if institutions control backtest leakage, false discoveries, liquidity shocks, default clustering, and systemic coordination risks"
   Interview with Marcos Lopez de Prado and Alexander Lipton of Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (2026)

7. Backtests over many assets usually make a strategy look more skilled than it is, so the plain Sharpe ratio cannot be trusted; correct it.
   > "Multi-asset backtests often overstate skill due to overfitting and hidden risks, making standard Sharpe ratios unreliable."
   > "I recommend causal design and statistical corrections like deflated Sharpe and false discovery controls to assess if performance is genuine."
   Interview with Marcos Lopez de Prado of Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA), JPM 52(5) (2026)

8. A model built on the wrong cause and effect can lose money steadily even when its correlations hold; they call this worse than p-hacking (testing until something looks good).
   > "specification errors are arguably more dangerous to investors than p -hacking"
   Why Has Factor Investing Failed?: The Role of Specification Errors / Correcting the Factor Mirage: A Research Protocol for Causal Factor Investing (2026)

9. Thinking in cause and effect instead of in correlations gives sturdier strategies and fewer false discoveries.
   > "practitioners can build more robust strategies, reduce false discoveries, and restore trust in factor-based approaches"
   Causality and Factor Investing: A Primer (2025)

10. Machine learning has worked in ten named places in finance, but still has real problems.
    > "This article describes ten notable financial applications where ML has moved beyond hype and proven its usefulness."
    > "This success does not mean that the use of ML in finance does not face important challenges."
    Ten Applications of Financial Machine Learning (2025)

11. Use machine learning for small edges, but beware overfitting and models you cannot see inside.
    > "I highlight flaws of traditional econometrics, warn against overfitting and black-box models, and emphasize the importance of causal inference."
    Interview with Marcos Lopez de Prado of Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA), JPM 51(8) (2025)

12. Weights picked from a noisy estimate of risk are themselves noisy; the HRP way of weighting is less noisy than Markowitz's.
    > "We demonstrate that the HRP is less noisy (and thus more robust) than the classical Markowitz approach."
    Overcoming Markowitz's Instability with the Help of Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP): Theoretical Evidence (2025)

13. Machine learning can be added to classic portfolio building for finding edge, for risk, and for limiting bad-case losses.
    > "we show how to enhance portfolio management processes including alpha generation, risk management, and optimization of risk metrics like conditional value at risk"
    Enhancing Markowitz's Portfolio Selection Paradigm with Machine Learning (2025)

14. There are three main kinds of backtest, and practitioners should use the more reliable ones.
    > "reviewing the three principal types of backtests: Walk-forward, Resampling, and Monte Carlo"
    The Three Types of Backtests (Enhanced Backtesting for Practitioners) (2024)

15. Papers report the chance a result is luck but not the chance the test would have caught a real edge; this gives both, also for a whole family of tests at once.
    > "Most papers in the financial literature estimate the p -value associated with an investment strategy, without reporting the power of the test used to make that discovery."
    > "These estimates allow researchers to carefully design experiments with high confidence and power."
    Confidence and Power of the Sharpe Ratio under Multiple Testing (Type I and Type II Errors of the Sharpe Ratio under Multiple Testing) (2022)

16. Every step of classic statistics in finance has a matching step in machine learning.
    > "This article demonstrates that each analytical step of the econometric process has a homologous step in ML analyses."
    Beyond Econometrics: A Roadmap Towards Financial Machine Learning (2022)

17. There are two kinds of false ('spurious') claims, and one kind keeps the field from moving forward.
    > "I differentiate between type-A and type-B spurious claims"
    Causal Factor Investing: Can Factor Investing Become Scientific? (working paper, 2022)

18. Published strategies are often paid-for and never corrected for how many things were tried.
    > "Commercial investment firms pay researchers for publishing academic articles that promote dubious investment strategies, without controlling for multiple testing."
    How 'Backtest Overfitting' in Finance Leads to False Discoveries (2021)

19. The best Sharpe ratio rises with the number of strategies tried, so no fixed Sharpe bar can prove a strategy is real.
    > "With enough number of trials, any false strategy may achieve a Sharpe ratio as high as a researcher demands."
    The False Strategy Theorem: A Financial Application of Experimental Mathematics (2021)

20. Some measures built from how trades happen predict market numbers on days the model did not see.
    > "We find that some microstructure-based measures are useful for out-of-sample prediction of various market statistics, leading to questions about the efficiency of markets."
    Microstructure in the Machine Age (2021)

21. For prices that swing back to an average, the best take-profit, stop-loss and longest hold can be worked out by formula.
    > "we develop an analytical framework that derives the optimal trading levels of profit-taking, stop-losses, and maximum investment horizon"
    A Closed-Form Solution for Optimal Onrstein-Uhlenbeck driven trading strategies (2020) (the page's own spelling)

22. The same idea for a plain 'buy low, sell back at the average' strategy.
    > "A mean-reverting trading strategy can monetize this oscillation at appropriate profit-taking, stop-loss and maximum-horizon levels."
    A Closed-Form Solution for Optimal Mean-Reverting Trading Strategies (2020)

23. Three ways to test a trading rule on history, each with pros and cons.
    > "There are three fundamental ways of testing the validity of an investment algorithm against historical evidence: a) the walk-forward method; b) the resampling method; and c) the Monte Carlo method."
    Tactical Investment Algorithms (2020)

24. A strategy with no reason behind it is probably false; spend the effort on the reason, not on backtesting more rules.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

**Costs.** Covered only in a few summaries, and with no sizes. Trading too much and fills worse than planned can eat a strategy's profit ("the costs from excessive turnover and implementation shortfall may critically erode the profitability of their investment strategies"). Your own market impact depends on whether you trade with or against the crowd, and cost models rarely count that (Optimal Execution Horizon, 2015). A multi-period plan counts "permanent and temporary market impact" (2016). One paper explains the buy-sell gap (the spread) from trade data (2016). No fee rates or spread figures are given.

**Overfitting.** The main theme. Trying many versions and keeping the best makes results look good even when nothing is real; a few tries are enough (2014); with enough tries any false strategy reaches any Sharpe ratio (2021); there is a way to estimate the chance a backtest is overfit, PBO with CSCV (2017); the Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for it (2014, 2026); you cannot tell the false share from the tested days' numbers alone (2026); report every test you ran (2018).

**Testing on unseen data.** Portfolios tuned on the past "perform erratically on more recent, out-of-sample data" (2017). It names three ways to test: walk-forward, resampling and Monte Carlo (2020, 2024), but the page does not say which is best or how to set them up. Some trade-flow measures did predict on unseen data (2021). HRP weights did better than classic optimisation on unseen data in simulations (2016).

**Position size.** The best bet sizes for a risk-averse trader with a fixed time span (2019); smaller allocations once hidden risks count (2003); HRP and the "robust estimator" for steadier portfolio weights (2016, 2019, 2025). No sizing figures.

**Leverage.** Mentioned once, for private equity, not trading: leverage drives "both optimal investment and pricing" (2026). Nothing on trading leverage, margin or liquidation.

**Stops.** For prices that swing back to an average, the best take-profit, stop-loss and longest hold can be worked out by formula (2020, two papers; 2014 without a closed formula). When to switch off a strategy after a loss or a long time below its high, and the "triple penance" rule (2015). No stop sizes are given.

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. **The best of many tries looks good by luck alone (False Strategy Theorem).** Take the random-entry strategies this repo already measures (the luck bar of the Pro Trader and confluence research, `pt_rand` / `cq_rn`), draw groups of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 of them, and plot the best profit a trade (or best Sharpe) in each group against the group size. If the claim holds, the best of a bigger group is higher even though every member is random. Then put the real grid's best rows on the same chart: a real edge would sit clearly above the random curve for the same number of tries.
2. **Shrink the best rows for the number tried (Deflated Sharpe Ratio).** For each coin-timeframe, count the combinations tried (Backtest v2 measures every signal x stop x target) and correct the top row's Sharpe for that count and for how lopsided its trade results are. The page names DSR but gives no formula, so the formula must come from the paper itself. Expected if the claim holds: few or none of the rows survive, which fits this repo's own finding that picks made on older days lost money on newer days.
3. **The chance a backtest is overfit (PBO with CSCV).** Cut each coin-timeframe's 30 days into, say, 8 blocks; for every way of picking half the blocks as 'tested', choose the best row on those and see where it ranks on the other half. The share of splits where it ranks below the middle is the overfitting chance. A share near half or above means choosing is no better than random.
4. **Tuned-on-the-past does worse on newer data (2017).** Already partly seen here: the Nov 05, 2026 walk-forward over 12,527,659 v2 rows found every way of choosing lost money on the newest 15 days. Repeat on the next nights' stores to check it is not one store's accident.
5. **Triple penance rule.** For each strategy's trade list, measure how long the worst drop took to happen and how long it took to climb back to the old high. The claim says the climb is about three times the fall (under its own assumptions); check the ratio across thousands of strategies.
6. **Lopsided results inflate the Sharpe ratio (PSR).** Rows with a wide target and tight stop have lopsided trade results. Compare their plain Sharpe with a version adjusted for that, and see whether rankings change.
7. **The shortest record that shows skill (MinTRL).** Compare the trade counts the rooms require (20, 30 or 50 trades) with the minimum track record the paper's measure would ask for, given each row's own Sharpe and shape of results.
8. **Bell-curve risk understates losses (2004).** Compare each strategy's real worst losing streak with what independent coin flips at its own win rate would predict; if losses cluster, the real streaks are longer.
9. **Best take-profit and stop by formula (2020).** On coins whose prices swing back to an average, fit the swing and compute the formula's best target, stop and hold; compare with the grid's best target and stop on the same coin. Agreement would let us skip part of the grid; disagreement says the formula's assumptions do not fit crypto.
10. **Trading with or against the crowd changes your cost (2015).** The app records each contract's order book every minute. Check whether the cost a fill paid is bigger when the book is leaning the same way as the order.
11. **The false share cannot be read from the tested days (2026).** Compare, over Stored strategies, what share of rows 'made money' on the days they were chosen on with the share that made money on the unseen days; a big drop is what the claim predicts.

## What I did not read, and doubts

- **I read only the summaries on this one page, not one paper.** Every lesson above is the author's own two-to-four sentence summary. None of the measures (DSR, PSR, MinTRL, PBO, CSCV, Bayesian FDR, HRP) is explained on the page, so this note cannot say how to compute any of them; that needs the papers at the links.
- I opened none of the links, so I do not know which papers are free, which links still work, or whether the SSRN versions match the journal versions.
- Some summaries are not findings: the book entries (Advances in Financial Machine Learning, the 2016 book chapter) are blurbs, and the interviews are opinions.
- The saved text has broken characters where the page used accents or curly quotes (for example in the accented spelling of López, in Markowitz's CLA, and around the words factor mirage); I left those passages out of the quotes. The page also has its own typos ("Onrstein", "discirimnate", "systemtically"), quoted as written.
- Several 2026 entries are marked "Forthcoming" and the page gives volume numbers for others (e.g. JPM Vol. 53); I took the page's dates and volumes as written and did not check them.
- The triple penance rule's "three times" holds "Under standard portfolio theory assumptions", in the page's own words; crypto trade results may not meet those assumptions.
- The page says most of the work is private, so the published list is not the whole of his research.
- Which papers I counted as 'about trading' is my own judgement from the summaries; a few borderline ones (the HRP and robust-weights papers as sizing, the spread paper as costs) are flagged in their lessons.
