# Technical Papers (John Ehlers, MESA Software)

- Link: https://mesasoftware.com/TechnicalArticles.htm
- Type: html (one web page: a list of 53 downloadable articles plus one book correction, each with a short blurb)
- Free or paid: free (every article is a free download link on the author's site; the page states no price, though it names the author as "the developer of MESA", his own product)
- Words read of total: 2,768 of 2,768 (the whole text file; the 53 articles behind the links were NOT read, only their blurbs)

## What it is

A single page on John Ehlers's own website that lists his technical articles about indicators and filters, each with a title, a blurb of one to six sentences, and a download link with a file size. It opens with a correction for his book "Cybernetic Trading Indicators", then the list, roughly newest first (the newest-looking items mention a 2023 interview and his 100th article; the oldest-looking ones at the bottom are about Fisher transforms, Gaussian filters and poles and zeros). Almost every blurb makes a claim about an indicator (less lag, fewer whipsaws, better prediction), but the page itself gives no test, no trade, no profit figure and no cost figure for any of them. Three entries are about testing a strategy rather than building an indicator: Walk Forward Optimization, a robustness procedure, and two Monte Carlo spreadsheets built from Profit Factor and win rate.

> "John Ehlers, the developer of MESA, has written and published many papers relating to the principles used in market cycles."

Where: the paragraph under the heading "John Ehlers' TECHNICAL PAPERS Available for Download".

## Everything it teaches

Every item on the page, in page order (1 is the book correction, 2 to 54 are the articles). Each lesson is only what the title and blurb say; the articles themselves were not read.

1. Errata for his book: ignore chapter 19's dominant-cycle method, because it assumed a clean sine wave and fails on noisy prices; measure the cycle from how fast the phase angle changes (chapter 18) instead.
> "Please disregard Chapter 19 and the $DominantCycle function."
> "This approach just does not work for noisy market data."
> "A more robust solution is to compute the Dominant Cycle from the average rate change of phase angle computed in Chapter 18."
Where: the very top of the page, the ERRATA note for the book Cybernetic Trading Indicators, with the link Dominant Cycle, download code 37Kb.

2. A New Look at Prediction: you can only predict a few bars ahead reliably; longer guesses need a price model that assumes the pattern holds for a short while.
> "Reliable predictions can only project a few bars into the future."
> "Longer term predictions can be made using a price model that assumes a short term coherence."
Where: first article on the list.

3. The Autotune Filter: a filter that lets through one cycle length, retuned all the time to the market's main cycle, which it finds from the autocorrelation (how much prices look like their own past).
> "a Bandpass filter whose center period is dynamically tuned to the Dominant Cycle period, which is determined by minimizing the autocorrealtion function"
Where: second article. ("autocorrealtion" is the page's own spelling.)

4. The Jack and Jill Indicator: a trend indicator made of two lines, a fast one that hugs price and a slow one with the cycles taken out.
> "Jack and Jill is a trend indicator."
> "It is comprised of two lines: "Jack" that closely follows the price action, and "Jill" that is virtually devoid of cyclic information."
Where: third article.

5. The Adaptive SuperSmoother: he says the usual adaptive average (an EMA whose speed follows volatility) is a poor filter, and offers a second-order filter tuned to how fast the data changes.
> "Most adaptive filters are simple EMAs with the alpha term modified by volatility."
> "The EMA is just not a very good filter."
> "The Adaptive SuperSmoother is a second order filter tuned to the data rate of change."
Where: fourth article.

6. The Reversion Index: add up the last few bar-to-bar price changes and divide by the sum of their sizes, so it reads near +1 or -1 when moves all go one way.
> "The Reversion Index is simply the short-term sum of the bar-to-bar price differences normalized to the absolute value of those price differences."
Where: fifth article. The near +1 or -1 reading is my arithmetic from the definition, not the page's words; the page gives no length or threshold.

7. The Continuation Index: claimed to warn early when a trend starts and when it runs out.
> "The Continuation Index provides early indications of trend onsets and exhaustion."
Where: sixth article.

8. Laguerre Filters: claimed to be very good at smoothing out the slow parts of price, which he says makes them suited to trend trading.
> "Laguerre Filters have an exceptional capability for smoothing long wavelength components in the data spectrum."
> "This makes them an ideal candidate as a tool for trend trading."
Where: seventh article.

9. Drunkard's Walk: describes random market data with the "drunkard's walk" (random steps) idea.
> "Random market data is described in terms of the Drunkard's Walk algorithm."
Where: eighth article.

10. Removing Moving Average Lag: push the average forward to the newest bar by extending it along the slope of the data.
> "Moving Average lag is removed by projecting the average to the right hand side of the window along the data slope."
Where: ninth article.

11. The One Euro Filter: a simple, rough adaptive filter.
> "The One Euro Filter is a quick and dirty adaptive filter"
Where: tenth article.

12. Kalman and Alpha-Beta Filters: two tracking filters, explained and compared.
> "Kalman and Alpha-Beta filters are described and compared."
Where: eleventh article.

13. Linear Predictive Filters: he says these filters really can predict, better as they are tuned narrower, and describes the Griffiths method, which keeps adjusting the filter to shrink the error between the last price and its prediction.
> "Linear predictive (all-pole) filters actually have a predictive capability."
> "The prediction improves as the bandwidth is narrowed."
> "It computes linear predictive filter coefficients by adaptively minimizing the error between the last data point and the prediction."
Where: twelfth article. Its link is labelled "Limited Predictive Filters" while the heading and file name say "Linear".

14. The Ultimate Oscillator (his version): almost no lag; it is the difference between two high-pass filters (filters that keep only the fast moves) with different cut-off lengths.
> "The Ultimate Oscillator has almost no lag."
> "It is created by taking the difference of two second order Highpass filters having different critical periods."
Where: thirteenth article.

15. Ultimate Channel and Ultimate Bands: price channels and bands built on his Ultimate Smoother, claimed to have very little lag.
> "The Ultimate Smoother is used to create price channels and price bands having extremely small lag."
Where: fourteenth article.

16. The Ultimate Smoother: he claims it beats moving averages because it smooths with very little lag; the article gives EasyLanguage code and reviews his SuperSmoother and bandpass filters on the way.
> "The UltimateSmoother is vastly superior to conventional smoothers such as moving averages because the smoothing is done with very little lag."
> "The UltimateSmoother is derived and fully described in EasyLanguage code."
> "The SuperSmoother and Bandpass filters are reviewed in the derivation process."
Where: fifteenth article.

17. An interview with him in Stocks & Commodities magazine (April 2023) on the thinking behind using signal processing in trading.
> "This interview by Kevin Davies touches on many of the philosophical underpinnings of using the science of Digital Signal Processing (DSP) in the art of trading."
Where: sixteenth item, "John Ehlers Interview in the April 2023 issure of Stocks & Commodities Magazine" (sic), linked to Traders.com, not to his site.

18. Just Ignore Them: more data points are not more accuracy; you can build a smoothing filter by skipping bars (downsampling).
> "Don't confuse data resolution and accuracy."
> "This artile shows how to make a smoothing filter by downsampling."
Where: seventeenth article. ("artile" is the page's own spelling.)

19. Every Little Bit Helps: use the average of each bar's open and close as the price, which he says removes a little lag and blocks the fastest possible wiggle (the two-bar cycle).
> "Using the average of Open and Close as the data sample has the advantages of removing a small amount of lag and providing a zero of transmission at the Nyquist frequency if your indicator does not already have one."
Where: eighteenth article. "Zero of transmission at the Nyquist frequency" is signal-processing language; my plain reading is that it lets none of the 2-bar wiggle through.

20. Recurring Phase of Cycle Analysis: his 100th article for Stocks & Commodities; it looks at how a cycle's position relates to its speed, and at how cycle (sideways) and trend periods relate.
> "On the occasion of the 40th anniversary of Stocks & Commodities Magazine, John Ehlers wrote his 100th article for them."
> "He examines the relationship between the cycle phase and its frequency."
> "He also analyzes the relationship between the cycle mode and trend mode in the data."
Where: nineteenth article.

21. Pairs Rotation: switch between a stock and an index, or between sector funds, holding whichever is stronger; he claims this cuts the drop in the account and grows it, using his "Ehlers Loops" for timing.
> "Drawdown is minimized by rotating between a stock and an index, depending on which is stronger."
> "Equity growth can be maximized by rotating between Sector ETFs, depending on which is stronger."
> "Using Ehlers Loops gives you the means to effect rotation timing with maximum effectiveness."
Where: twentieth article.

22. Ehlers Loops: plot price against volume, both measured in standard deviations (how unusual each is), and he claims a hand trader can then reliably predict the next price move.
> "Plotting price versus volume, where both data sets are scaled in terms of standard deviations, provides the discretionary trader with a means to reliably predict future price action."
Where: twenty-first article.

23. (Yet Another) Improved RSI: an RSI that runs from -1 to +1 around zero instead of 0 to 100, smoothed with a Hann window (a weighting that is heavier in the middle).
> "This RSI swings between -1 and +1 and has a nominal zero mean."
> "The indicator is natively smoothed using Hann Windowing."
Where: twenty-second article.

24. An Improved Directional Movement Indicator: he says the old directional movement indicator is good but carries habits from the pencil-and-paper days, and updates it for computer trading.
> "Directional Movement has been part of Technical Analysis for five decades, invented using pencil and paper for its calculations."
> "Directional Movement is actually a pretty good indicator, but it carries a lot of baggage due to the available technology at the time it was created."
> "It is time to freshen it up for use in modern algorithmic trading."
Where: twenty-third article.

25. A Thinking Man's MACD: an MACD made from two fixed-window filters, with a stated reason for their lengths, and no third smoothing line needed.
> "there is a rationale to establish the lengths of the filters"

Where: twenty-fourth article.

26. Windowing: simple moving averages are everywhere but are poor filters; easy changes to them give a near-best balance of smoothing against lag.
> "Simple Moving Averages (SMA) are ubiquitous in technical analysis."
> "However, the truth is that they are not very good filters."
> "The paper presents simple, easy to program, modifications that represent a near optimum compromise between filtering and lag for their use in technical analysis of the market."
Where: twenty-fifth article.

27. A Technical Description of Market Data for Traders: he describes prices as random noise whose character changes over time; taking bar-to-bar changes exposes swings in size that follow volatility, and clipping and re-adding them gives a timing signal centred on zero, like a radio FM receiver.
> "Market data is a nonstationary random process that has a pink noise spectrum."
> "The spectrum can be whitened by taking a derivative."
> "This exposes the amplitude modulation in the waveform, which is correlated to volatility."
> "The amplitude modulation can be stripped by hard limiting the waveform, and then integrating that waveform."
> "This process is basically the same as an FM demodulator of radio signals."
Where: twenty-sixth article (file AMFM.pdf).

28. Noise Elimination Technology: clean fast noise out of an indicator with Kendall correlation (a rank-based correlation) instead of a smoothing filter.
> "Remove high frequency noise from indicators using Kendall Correlation."
> "This rank ordered correlation nonlinearly clarifies your indicators rather than using filters"
Where: twenty-seventh article.

29. Truncating Indicators: an EMA, MACD and similar indicators give a different answer depending on how much history you start them on; cutting them to a fixed window removes that start-up problem.
> "The Exponential Moving Average (EMA), and consequently the MACD and other indicators, are Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) types of filters."
> "The answer your get from an IIR filter will be different depending on your data length."
> "If the data stream is sufficiently long the answer may be the same for all practical purposes, but it will be different nonetheless."
> "So, initialization is one problem that is resolved by truncating the indicators"
Where: twenty-eighth article (heading "Truncating Indicators", link "Truncated Indicators"). ("your get" is the page's own wording.)

30. Correlation as a Cycle Indicator: claimed to stay fairly accurate even with the wrong cycle length; it breaks down when the market trends, and he uses that breakdown to tell trend from sideways; a "phasor angle" display is said to show the right position in both.
> "Correlation as a cycle indicator is robust, yielding only relatively small errors even if an incorrect judgement is made in assigning the dominant cycle to the indicator."
> "However, the cycle mode indicator fails when the market enters a trend mode."
> "But that failure can be used to rapidly identify the current market mode."
> "The Phasor angle display indicates the correct trade position for either the cycle mode or the trend mode."
Where: twenty-ninth article.

31. Correlation as a Trend Indicator: correlate recent prices with a rising straight line; near +1 means an up trend, near -1 a down trend.
> "Just imagine correlating prices with a straight line having a positive slope."
> "If the price trend is up, the correlation is nearly +1."
> "If the price trend is down, there is anticorrelation and the correlation is nearly -1."
Where: thirtieth article.

32. Deviation Scaled Moving Average (DSMA): an EMA whose speed is set by how far an oscillator sits from its mean, in standard deviations, so it speeds up when price moves sharply.

39. Predictive Indicators for Effective Trading Strategies: smoothing causes lag; and because the price swing grows with the length of the bar (a weekly bar swings more than a daily one, roughly in proportion), indicators get distorted, which he calls "spectral dilatation" and says others ignore.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

Costs: not covered. No blurb mentions fees, spread, slippage or funding, and no trade is priced anywhere on the page.

Overfitting: only touched on. The Walk Forward Optimization blurb says a plain backtest predicts the future poorly ("It is common knowledge that simple backtesting can provide a poor probability of future trading results."), and the robustness blurb promises "a way to identify the optimization range of input parameters". Neither blurb says how; the method is inside PDFs that were not read.

Testing on unseen data: mentioned twice. Walk-forward is "out of sample testing is done a little at a time", with the warning "However WFO testing is not without its own problems." The System Evaluation blurb says the Monte Carlo spreadsheet should be fed Profit Factor and win rate measured "(out of sample)". No numbers are given.

Position size: not covered. The nearest thing is Pairs Rotation, which is about which of two things to hold, not how much ("Drawdown is minimized by rotating between a stock and an index, depending on which is stronger."), and the CoinToss spreadsheet's "portfolio trading" statistics, which are not described.

Leverage: not covered.

Stops: not covered. Several blurbs talk about entry and exit signals ("precise identification of the correct trade entry and exit points", "excellent entry and exit signals"), but none mentions a stop loss or a target.

## Claims we could test on our own data

Every test below has to follow this repo's own rules: Gate's 0.075% fee each way plus each minute's recorded order book and funding, the setting chosen on older days and judged on newer days it never saw, every coin and every timeframe (15m, 30m, 1h, 4h, 1d), and a random-entry luck bar beside it. The repo's earlier research (Pro Trader, Oct 20, 2026; confluence, Oct 22, 2026) found picks about level with random entries before fees, so any Ehlers indicator has to beat that, not just make a nice chart. The page gives no lengths or thresholds, so each test would have to try a small fixed set of settings decided before looking at results.

1. Claim: Fisher transform gives precise entries (lessons 51, 52). The grid already has a `fisher` signal (`tradingagents/signals_ext2.py`, length 10, turning back from beyond ±1.5). Its stored Backtest v2 rows can be read today and compared with the luck bar. The Inverse Fisher oscillator is not in the grid and would be a new signal; its claim "without whipsaws" can be counted directly (number of flips per 100 bars against `stochrsi` or `rsi14`).
2. Claim: MAMA/FAMA crosses are almost free of whipsaws (lesson 48). Build MAMA/FAMA as a cross signal and compare, on the same coins, the number of crosses, the win rate and the money after costs with the grid's `emacross` and `kama10` (the grid's existing adaptive average).
3. Claim: Correlation with a straight line is a trend indicator (lesson 31). Easy to build from the blurb alone: the correlation of the last N closes with the bar number; go long above a level near +1, short below one near -1. Compare with the grid's `lrslope` (slope of a line, which is a related but different measure) and `trend50`.
4. Claim: The Reversion Index (lesson 6). Also buildable from the blurb: sum of the last N bar-to-bar changes divided by the sum of their sizes; fade it at extremes like `zscore20` and `rsi2`.
5. Claim: Every Little Bit Helps: use (open + close) / 2 (lesson 19). A clean A/B test on signals already in the grid: run, say, `emacross`, `macd`, `rsi14` and `fisher` once on the close and once on (open + close) / 2, same everything else, and compare the unseen-day money. This changes only the input, so any difference is the input's.
6. Claim: Truncating indicators: an EMA's answer depends on how much history it starts on (lesson 29). This one matters to this repo directly: the runner reads 299 closed candles while the backtest reads the whole history, which is why the Pro Trader indicators were built with fixed windows (`pro_indicators`, with no recursion). Test: for each EMA-based signal in the grid (`emacross`, `macd`, `trix15`, `kama10` and others), compute its signals on the last 299 candles only and on the full history, and count the candles where the two disagree. Any disagreement is a trade the backtest measured that the runner would not take.
7. Claim: His Ultimate Oscillator has almost no lag (lesson 14). Note the name clash: the grid's `ultosc` is a different indicator (a weighted mix of 7, 14 and 28-bar buying pressure, per its code), not the two-high-pass-filter version this page describes. Building Ehlers's version would need a new name. Lag can be measured without any trading: shift the indicator against a known slow cycle in made-up data and see how many bars behind it turns.
8. Claim: Improved RSI, improved directional movement, thinking man's MACD (lessons 23, 24, 25). Each has an old version already in the grid (`rsi14`, `adx14`, `macd`), so each can be a head-to-head pair: old and new, same coins, same exits, same costs.
9. Claim: SuperSmoother, Ultimate Smoother, Laguerre, Gaussian, DSMA, FRAMA, Zero Lag smoothers beat moving averages (lessons 5, 8, 15, 16, 32, 41, 47, 49, 53). Use each as the average inside the same crossover or price-versus-average rule and compare with `emacross` / `trend50`. The claim is less lag at the same smoothness, which can also be measured directly on made-up data, before any trading test.
10. Claim: Walk-forward beats a plain backtest and Monte Carlo from Profit Factor and win rate (lessons 36, 38, 46). The repo already judges on unseen days, and the skill checks already include a check that 80%+ of 200 Monte Carlo draws are in profit. A cheap check: take Backtest v2 rows, draw account curves from only their older-days Profit Factor and win rate (as the spreadsheet does), and see whether that predicts their newest 15 days better than random.
11. Claim: Pairs rotation into the stronger of two (lesson 21). The v3 strength family already ranks coins weekly by strength; a two-asset version (a coin against BTC, hold the stronger) could be tested on the same plan.
12. Claim: Correlation as a cycle indicator tells trend from sideways and Empirical Mode Decomposition detects cycle and trend modes (lessons 30, 42). A mode detector can be tested as a filter: does a trend rule do better on bars it calls "trend", and a reversal rule on bars it calls "cycle", than on the others, after costs?
