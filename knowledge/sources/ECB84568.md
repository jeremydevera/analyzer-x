## What it is

This is the opening of Kevin Davey's book, given away as a sample: the Wiley series blurb, the title and copyright pages, the full table of contents, the acknowledgments, the Introduction (book pages 1-5) and Chapter 1, "The Birth of a Trader" (book pages 9-14). The Introduction tells the story of a December 2003 live cattle trade that lost about seven times what he had planned to lose, says who the book is for, defines "strategy", "mechanical" and "hybrid" trading, and lays out the book's seven parts. Chapter 1 is his beginner story from 1989: a mail-order head and shoulders course he sent back, then a hand-calculated triple moving average system on live hogs that lost 30% of a $5,000 account, then its "opposite" that took the loss to 60%. The excerpt does NOT contain any of his actual development process: limited testing, walk-forward analysis, Monte Carlo, incubation, position sizing and diversification appear only as chapter names in the table of contents. Every lesson below is either a warning drawn from his own early mistakes, a short statement in the Introduction, or a chapter name.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

Costs: only lessons from his mistakes, no numbers. Trading for real taught him slippage, broker mistakes and how slow phoned-in orders are ("I los t more t han I ma de, an d I learne d /f_i rst han d a bout slippage, bro ker ’s errors"), and he calls it "twisted reasonin g" that he treated the commission and slippage of a losing system as gains for its reverse. The point that costs are paid in both directions is the closest the excerpt comes to a cost rule. No cost per trade or cost model is given.

Overfitting: covered as a warning, not a method. Losing money right after tuning a strategy to the past means the process was wrong; a rule should be walk-forward tested and only "gent ly optimized"; a test of 10 or 20 trades is too small, hundreds are needed; and a single profitable chart example proves nothing because one can be found on almost any chart. The chapters that would explain how to avoid it (limited testing, walk-forward, Monte Carlo) are only named in the contents.

Testing on unseen data: the idea is there, the method is not. He says to judge a pattern with its outcome hidden, that he paper traded every head and shoulders signal for a month (a forward test on new prices) and found most of them lost, and that a moving average cross can be walk-forward tested. ## Claims we could test on our own data

1. Losing money right after optimizing (lessons 25, 27). Take the best rows chosen on older days and see what they make on the newer days they were not chosen on. The Backtest v2 store already holds each row's older and newest 15 days (`t15`/`w15`/`p15`), and Backtest v3 keeps older walks and 90 unseen days; compare the chosen rows' unseen profit after fees with the average row and with random entries.

2. Ten or twenty trades are not enough, hundreds are needed (lesson 62). For every v2 row, rank by the first 20 trades and then by the first 200, and measure how often the sign of profit (and the win rate band) holds on the trades that come after. A bootstrap of a row's own trades gives the spread of its win rate at 20 trades versus 200.

3. The simplest moving average rule "works very well during prolonged trends, but gets absolutely hammered" in sideways markets (lesson 55). Split the trades of the price-above-average and crossover signals by the market's state when they opened (for example BTC's mood in v3, or a trend strength reading) and compare profit a trade after fees in trending and sideways stretches.

4. Two or three averages filter out whipsaw losses (lessons 56-58). On the same coins and timeframes, compare one-average, two-average and three-average crossover rules: trade count, share of short back-and-forth losers, and profit a trade after fees.

5. Head and shoulders "gives a lot of false signals" and taking every one goes broke (lessons 46, 50). Build a detector that only uses closed candles, take every signal, and compare it with random entries on the same candles (the luck bar this repo already uses), before and after fees.

6. Reversing a losing system does not make a winner because costs are paid both ways (lessons 76, 77). Flip the side of every trade of a set of losing rows and re-measure with the same fees and order-book costs: if the original is near zero before costs, both the original and its reverse should lose about the round-trip cost each trade.

7. Abandoning a trend system after a few losses misses the trend (lessons 75, 79, 81). Simulate a rule that switches a strategy off, or over to its opposite, after K losses in a row, against simply keeping it running, on the same trade lists, and compare the totals after fees.

8. A planned stop is not a guaranteed exit; the real loss was about 7 times the planned one (lessons 12, 13). In the minute data, measure each stopped trade's real loss against its planned stop (a stop the price jumped past fills where the price was, as Backtest v3 already books it), and report the worst ratio and how often a fill is worse than the stop. Liquidation at 20x is the crypto version of being unable to exit.

9. One position can swing 25 percent of the account in an hour (lesson 69). Measure, per trade, the worst move against the position within any hour, as a share of the $5 margin at 20x.

10. Paper trading every signal reveals what picked charts hide (lesson 51). Compare the practice rooms' trades with the backtest rows they came from, trade by trade, as the agreement checks already do.
