Three posts are shown whole (the LLM test, the Poor Person's Transformer and the book introduction); the other five are only their first paragraph, with a "Read more" link to the author's Substack. Almost everything is about machine learning and generative AI as tools for traders (feature selection, attention, transformers, PCA), not about a tested trading rule; the one concrete strategy on the page (a VIX futures trade) appears inside a ChatGPT answer that the author quotes. The side bar lists the author's books, paid courses and workshops, his fund QTS, and a blog archive running from 2006 to 2025.

## Everything it teaches

Post "Deep Latent Variable Models" (Nov 27, 2025, excerpt only)

1. A latent variable model squeezes a list of inputs into a shorter summary that carries the same information.
   > "This encoding turns the feature vector X into a context vector z."
   Post: Deep Latent Variable Models.

2. These new-sounding AI models grow out of old tools traders already use, such as PCA and SVD.
   > "Latent variable models sound very GenAI-zy, but they descend from models that quant traders have long been familiar with."
   Post: Deep Latent Variable Models.

3. A hidden factor is worked out from the returns themselves, unlike a published factor you can look up.
   > "These variables are latent, or hidden, because they are inferred from the observed returns themselves, and not observable like the Fama-French factors such as HML or SMB."
   Post: Deep Latent Variable Models.

4. The point of hidden factors is that far fewer numbers have to be estimated.
   > "The benefit of applying these latent factors to model returns is that we need fewer parameters - i.e. dimensional reduction."
   Post: Deep Latent Variable Models.

5. You find the hidden factors either by breaking down the covariance table (PCA) or by breaking down the data table itself (SVD).
   > "The methods to find these latent factors are diagonalization of the covariance matrix in the PCA case, or singular value decomposition of the "design" (data) matrix in the SVD case."
   Post: Deep Latent Variable Models.

Post "Features Selection in the Age of Generative AI" (Oct 27, 2025, excerpt only)

6. "Features" are simply the inputs you feed a learning model, whether it predicts, groups or optimizes.
   > "Sometimes also called independent variables, covariates, or just X, they can be used for supervised or unsupervised learning, or for optimization."
   Post: Features Selection in the Age of Generative AI.

7. The author's own fund feeds more than 100 inputs into the choice of how to split money between two holdings.
   > "we use more than 100 of them as inputs to dynamically calibrate the allocation between our Tail Reaper strategy and E-mini SP 500 futures"
   Post: Features Selection in the Age of Generative AI.

8. Nobody knows ahead of time which inputs will help or which repeat each other.
   > "In general, modelers have no idea which features are useful a priori, or if they are redundant, for a particular application."
   Post: Features Selection in the Age of Generative AI.

9. Using every input you have makes a model fit the past and fail on new data, or even breaks the maths, so inputs must be chosen.
   > "Using all of the features can result in overfitting and poor out-of-sample performance, or worse, numerical instability and singularities during matrix inversion."
   Post: Features Selection in the Age of Generative AI.

Post "Book and Workshop Introduction: Generative AI for Trading & Asset Management" (Oct 03, 2025, shown whole; mostly an advert for the book and a workshop)

10. A "discriminative" model answers a yes/no style question, such as whether today will be up given yesterday.
    > "for example, given yesterday’s return, what is the probability that today’s return will be positive?"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

11. Reinforcement learning is used to place orders better and to split money across a portfolio.
    > "used for tasks like optimizing order execution or figuring out how to set the best capital allocations within a portfolio to get a better Sharpe ratio"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

12. Generative models can learn from price-return histories, not only from text and pictures.
    > "In particular, they can learn from time series of asset returns, which is perhaps the most relevant for asset managers."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

13. Chatbots are useful for chores: fetching adjusted prices, working out performance numbers, translating code, writing code from a plain description, and turning papers into code.
    > "Generating Python code based on plain-language descriptions of a strategy Summarizing research papers and turning the key ideas into code"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

14. A chatbot will not hand you a working money-maker.
    > "At this stage, GenAI does not provide reliable fully automated alpha generation."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

15. It can still help you draft and understand a strategy.
    > "We demonstrate examples where GenAI helped draft and understand: A long-short factor strategy A VIX futures carry strategy A SPX options calendar spread"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

16. These tools change quickly, so a test from early 2024 may already be out of date.
    > "Please, keep in mind that these models are evolving quickly. We tested these examples in early 2024."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

17. Guarding against overfitting and choosing between models are part of the basics.
    > "Techniques to avoid overfitting and improve model selection"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

18. Getting the data ready matters about as much as the model.
    > "Practical aspects of data engineering, which are often just as important as the model itself."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

19. You can make realistic fake price histories, and you must check how good they are.
    > "How to generate time series synthetic data, along with different methods to evaluate the quality of the generated data"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

20. Using both kinds of model together helps handle how unsure a forecast is.
    > "how combining the power of discriminative and generative models can help better handle uncertainty in predictions, a topic especially relevant in finance"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

21. Generative models also learn compact descriptions of data, which is what makes "RAG" (feeding a chatbot outside documents) work.
    > "They are also powerful tools for representation learning , which is key for techniques like Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) ."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

22. The four main generative model families (Transformers, VAEs, flow models, GANs) have to be adapted to how financial series behave.
    > "We explain how these models were originally developed and, more importantly, how to adapt them to the unique dynamics of financial time series"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

23. One worked system turns speech into trades: transcribe US Federal Reserve press conferences, score their mood with an LLM, trade on the score.
    > "We apply these techniques to analyze the sentiment of Federal Reserve (Fed) press conference speeches , generating trading signals based on that sentiment."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

24. Running these models costs money and time, worst of all for very fast trading, so speed matters.
    > "When it comes to real-world settings, you quickly run into practical constraints like cost and scalability for deploying these models, especially if you want to apply them for high-frequency trading."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

25. There are known ways to make a model cheaper and faster to run.
    > "Techniques for efficient inference, including Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), Knowledge distillation and Quantization."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

26. You can train a small open model further so it answers finance questions.
    > "building your own custom Finance LLM to answer finance-related questions by fine-tuning LLaMA 3.1 8B using QLoRA"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

27. Use common research tools so you can compare with others, but always test the idea on real market data too.
    > "and, just as importantly, test your ideas directly on financial data"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

28. Every result should be repeatable from the code that made it.
    > "We’ve also included a full code repository of examples for each chapter, focusing as much as possible on reproducibility."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

29. Knowing markets still matters most when a chatbot is your helper.
    > "emphasizing the critical role of domain expertise in designing meaningful trading strategies, particularly when using LLMs as copilots"
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

30. AI's success in other fields has not yet been matched in finance.
    > "We also discuss why GenAI, despite its success in other domains, may still require more empirical work to achieve similar results in finance."
    Post: Book and Workshop Introduction.

Post "Cross-Attention for Cross-Asset Applications" (Jun 06, 2025, excerpt only)

33. For one stock, 12 months of 52 inputs, each turned into 64 numbers, are laid end to end into one list of 768 numbers.
    > "768 is the result of 12 × 64: all the lagged features are concatenated / flattened into one vector."
    Post: Cross-Attention for Cross-Asset Applications.

34. "Cross-attention" lets one asset's data serve as background for another asset's.
    > "The purpose of cross-attention is that a feature in an asset i may be relevant as a context to a feature of an asset j."
    Post: Cross-Attention for Cross-Asset Applications.

35. It is the tool for many assets at once, to forecast their returns or split money between them.
    > "What if we have a portfolio of many stocks whose returns we want to predict, or whose allocations we want to optimize? That’s where cross-attention transformers come in."
    Post: Cross-Attention for Cross-Asset Applications.

Post "Applying Transformers to Financial Time Series" (May 23, 2025, excerpt only)

36. A transformer gives each reshaped input a weight for whatever uses it next.
    > "To be precise, the transformer assigns weights on the different transformed features for downstream applications."
    Post: Applying Transformers to Financial Time Series.

37. The next step is to add several kinds of inputs for one stock and keep their order in time.
    > "In this post, we will discuss how traders can incorporate different feature series from this stock while adding a sense of time."
    Post: Applying Transformers to Financial Time Series.

38. In the worked example, returns plus 51 company numbers make 52 columns, over 12 monthly snapshots.
    > "For concreteness, let’s say we use n=12 snapshots, captured at monthly intervals, and regard R(t) as the monthly return from t-1 to t. X is now a 12 × 52 matrix."
    Post: Applying Transformers to Financial Time Series.

Post "A Poor Person's Transformer" (May 16, 2025, shown whole)

39. Tree models choose their own inputs when you cap how big they can grow.
    > "Trees work very well with heterogeneous and tabular feature sets, and by limiting the number of nodes or the depth of a branch, there is feature selection by default."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

40. Older neural nets chose inputs with an "L1" penalty that pushes weights to zero, which gets clumsy with huge models.
    > "However, L1 regularizations are quite tedious when we have millions or billions of parameters in a deep neural network."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

41. Attention picks inputs separately for every example, unlike older methods that pick one set for all data.
    > "They aren’t selected globally like traditional feature selection methods do. In other words, the features are selected based on their values themselves."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

42. In the input table, the row count is the number of inputs, not the number of data samples.
    > "where n is the number of features ( not the number of samples!) and d is the dimension of each feature"
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

43. Each row can be a snapshot of a stock at one moment, going back n steps.
    > "So if you have n lookback periods, the feature matrix has dimension n × d."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

44. Q, K and V are three views of each input: what it looks for, what it offers, and itself in a new form.
    > "An element in Q is like “what this feature is looking for in other features that can provide as context”"
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

45. The mixing weights are learned once from the goal, but the attention score changes with each input.
    > "but the resulting attention score is computed as the function of the input sample X ."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

46. Q, K and V are like PCA mixes, done row by row so each moment in time stays separate.
    > "The intuition behind these Q, K, V is we want some linear mixtures of the original feature matrix X that best represent it, reminiscent of the familiar PCA."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

47. Scores are divided by the square root of the key size so they do not blow up, because the raw product's spread grows with that size.
    > "Then their dot product, has mean 0 and variance d k ."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

48. A softmax turns each row of scores into weights that add up to 1.
    > "Softmax function normalizes the scaled dot-product into a matrix where each row is the normalized weights (i.e. they sum to 1)"
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

49. The cheap version, with one number per input, just uses the inputs themselves for Q, K and V, and still gets a full n by n score table.
    > "It is still a n × n matrix formed by XX T ."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

50. To rate how important one input is, add up its column of the score table.
    > "you can sum over all the values of column j, since column j represents the key feature j"
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

51. The weighted inputs then go into a small neural net to forecast the next day's return, or into reinforcement learning.
    > "Downstream, we can use Z as input to a MLP for supervised learning, such as predicting the next day’s return, or for optimization via reinforcement learning."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

52. The author does not show that it works; he asks readers to try it.
    > "Does this work? You can ask ChatGPT or some other favorite chatbot to create a program based on this blog post and try it out."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

53. Mixed inputs (volatility, P/E, dividend) cannot simply be multiplied together; they need to be scaled and embedded first, and time order must be added.
    > "Unfortunately, the Poor Person’s self-attention method discussed above doesn’t work very well with features that cannot embedded in the same space."
    Post: A Poor Person's Transformer.

## What it says on costs, overfitting, testing on unseen data, position size, leverage and stops

Costs: Only twice. The VIX summary (written by ChatGPT, quoted by the author) says its test charged the whole buy-sell gap plus broker fees: "Transaction costs included full bid-ask spread + brokerage fees." The book post talks about the cost of RUNNING AI models ("practical constraints like cost and scalability"), which is computing cost, not trading cost. There is no fee figure, no slippage figure and no cost-against-target check anywhere on the page.

Overfitting: Covered as a reason to choose inputs: "Using all of the features can result in overfitting and poor out-of-sample performance". Tree models limit themselves by size, neural nets used an L1 penalty, and attention picks inputs per example. The book lists "Techniques to avoid overfitting and improve model selection" but the page does not say what they are.

Testing on unseen data: Mentioned, never shown with numbers. "poor out-of-sample performance" is the danger named in the feature-selection post. The VIX summary says hedge ratios were "updated daily out-of-sample" and that results held "out-of-sample and across sub-periods (pre/post-2009)". The LLM post flags a related trap: a benchmark may be unfair if the model was trained on the test ("this isn’t a completely fair benchmark"). The Poor Person's Transformer is never tested at all ("Does this work?").

Position size: Only as allocation, meaning how money is split. Reinforcement learning is used "to set the best capital allocations within a portfolio", QTS sets its split between Tail Reaper and E-mini S&P futures from 100+ inputs, and the VIX trade sizes its S&P hedge from a daily regression. No rule for how big one trade should be.

Leverage: not covered.

Stops: No price stop-loss anywhere. The only exit rules are the VIX trade's: leave when the signal fades past a line, or after a "Maximum holding period of 9 business days" (a time limit, not a price stop).

## Claims we could test on our own data

1. Claim: Annualize by x252, not by the square root. This is a reporting rule we can check in our own code: find every place a daily figure is turned into a yearly one and confirm it multiplies (not square-roots) a return, and that the day count matches our data. The page's 252 is for an exchange-traded fund (the ETF BILL); our coins trade every day, so the right count must be measured on our candles, not copied.
2. Claim: Using all inputs overfits and fails out of sample. Test on our learned formulas or any model: fit once with every input and once with a picked short list, both on the older days only, then compare profit a trade after fees on the newest unseen days.
3. Claim: A few hidden factors describe many assets' returns. Take one timeframe's returns across all coins, run PCA on the older half, and measure how much of the newer half's variation the first 1, 3 and 10 factors explain, and whether a 10-factor covariance is steadier on unseen days than the full table.
4. Claim: Given yesterday's return, what is the chance today is up? Straight count on our daily candles per coin: share of up days after an up day against the share of up days overall, older days against unseen days, and whether any gap survives our 0.075% fee each way plus the coin's gap.
5. Claim: Attention-weighted past returns (Z = AX) help predict the next bar. The author never tested it. Build the cheap version on each coin's last n returns, feed Z and the plain returns into the same small model, and compare next-bar forecasts on unseen days, then as trades after fees against random entries.
6. Claim: One asset's data helps forecast another's (cross-attention). Simplest version on our data: do BTC's last few returns add forecasting power for an alt coin's next bar beyond the alt's own, on unseen days? Our v3 research already has a BTC-mood filter to compare with.
7. Claim: The futures-to-spot gap predicts the futures, and profit comes from the gap closing. The page's claim is about VIX futures. The nearest thing in our data is the perpetual contract's funding rate; we could test whether a large funding rate predicts the perpetual's next price move (not only the funding paid), with every cost charged. This is an analogy, not the same claim.
8. Claim: A time limit on holding (9 trading days) is a sensible exit. Add a maximum hold to existing strategies and compare profit, win rate and worst losing run with and without it, chosen on older days and judged on unseen ones.
9. Claim: Hedging gives up a little profit and removes a lot of downside. Pair alt-coin trades with an opposite BTC position sized by a regression refit each day on past data only, and compare total profit and worst dip with and without the hedge, after the hedge's own fees.
10. Claim: A chatbot does not give reliable automatic alpha. Consistent with this repo's earlier research (searched picks no better than random entries); any chatbot-suggested rule should go through the same older-days-chosen, unseen-days-judged test with the random-entry bar.
